objections:
  - q: "I don't need God to be a good person."
    a: >-
      You're right that you can *know* right from wrong and *do* good things without
      believing in God. The Bible says God's law is written on every heart (Romans 2:15).
      The question isn't whether atheists can be good, but what makes "good" really good.
      You can drive a car without believing in its manufacturer — but the car still needed
      one. In the same way, you can be moral without believing in God, but morality still
      needs Him to exist.
  - q: "Morality is just a product of evolution. It helped our ancestors survive."
    a: >-
      Evolution might explain why we have certain *feelings* — like caring for our group.
      But it can't tell you whether those feelings are *true*. If morality is only a
      survival instinct, then "cruelty is wrong" is no more true than "sweet food tastes
      nice." And if a different instinct had helped survival — say, killing the weak —
      it would have been "moral." Most people sense that some things are wrong no matter
      what helped our ancestors survive.
  - q: "Different cultures have different morals, so morality is relative."
    a: >-
      Cultures do disagree about some things. But underneath, you find striking agreement:
      courage is honoured, betrayal is condemned, murder and theft are wrong. And
      disagreement doesn't prove there's no right answer — people once disagreed about
      whether the earth was round. The fact that we can say a culture got it *wrong* (like
      those that practised slavery) shows we believe in a standard above cultures.
  - q: "Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good?"
    a: >-
      This old puzzle (the Euthyphro dilemma) assumes only two options. There's a third:
      God commands what is good because He *is* good. Goodness isn't an arbitrary rule
      God made up, and it isn't a standard outside God that He has to follow. It flows
      from His own unchanging character. His commands are an expression of who He is.

Picture a university seminar. A confident student stands up and says, *"There's no such
thing as objective right and wrong. Morality is just what each person decides."*

Another student quietly walks over, picks up the first student's laptop, and starts
heading for the door with it.

*"Hey! That's mine! You can't do that!"*

*"Why not? I've decided it's right for me."*

It's a cheeky, made-up example — and you shouldn't actually take anyone's laptop! — but it makes a
powerful point. People may *say* morality is just personal preference. But the moment
someone wrongs them, they appeal to a standard that applies to everyone. In this lesson,
we'll see that this standard points to a **moral Lawgiver**.

## Two Kinds of "Right and Wrong"

To follow the argument, you need two simple words.

- **Subjective** — depends on the person (the *subject*). Taste in food, music and
  colours is subjective. *"Mint chocolate chip is the best ice cream"* is true for some
  people and false for others. Nobody is *wrong*.
- **Objective** — true whether anyone agrees or not. *"The earth goes round the sun"* is
  objective. It was true even when most people thought otherwise.

Now try this sentence: *"Torturing a child just for fun is wrong."*

Is that like an ice cream preference? Could someone say, *"Well, it's wrong for you, but
right for me"*? Almost everyone senses that's absurd. Some things are **really** wrong,
for everyone, everywhere, whether they agree or not. That's **objective morality**.

## The Moral Argument in Three Lines

William Lane Craig has popularised a simple form of this argument. Memorise it:

1. **If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.**
2. **Objective moral values and duties do exist.**
3. **Therefore, God exists.**

Let's walk through it.

### Line 1: Without God, there's no real right and wrong

If there's no God, then we are the accidental result of matter, time and chance. Our
moral feelings would just be chemical reactions and evolutionary instincts. They might
be useful, but they wouldn't be **true**.

Why would one bundle of chemicals owe anything to another bundle of chemicals? Who has
the authority to say, *"You **must** not do this"*? A **duty** (something you *ought* to
do) needs someone with the right to command it. Without God, there's nobody above us to
give moral commands — only opinions, feelings and social agreements.

Many thoughtful atheists have admitted this. That's why some of them conclude that
morality is ultimately an illusion. They're being consistent.

### Line 2: But real right and wrong do exist

Here's where most people's conscience takes over. We **know** that some things are truly
evil — genocide, racism, child abuse — and some things are truly good — self-sacrifice,
honesty, kindness to the weak.

Think about history's great moral reformers. William Wilberforce spent decades
fighting the British slave trade, even though it was legal and profitable. Martin
Luther King Jr. appealed to a moral law higher than the unjust laws of his day. We
honour them precisely because they were **right** when their societies were **wrong**.
But that only makes sense if there's a standard above every society, by which a whole
nation can be judged and found wanting. If morality were just majority opinion, the
reformers would have been the ones in the wrong.

We know this as clearly as we know that the world outside us is real. You can't prove
either with a science experiment, but you'd be foolish to deny them.

### Line 3: So God exists

If both lines are true, the conclusion follows. The best explanation for real moral
values is a **moral Lawgiver** whose own character is the standard of good. That's what
the Bible describes: a God who *is* good, and who made us in His image (Genesis 1:27) so
that we could know right from wrong.

> C.S. Lewis once used this very reasoning to escape atheism: you can't call a line
> *crooked* unless you have some idea of a *straight* line.

## "You Don't Need God to Be Good" — Knowing vs. Being

This is the reply you'll hear most often. It's worth understanding carefully, because
the person saying it is partly right.

There are two different questions:

- **How do we *know* right from wrong?** (Philosophers call this **epistemology** — the
  study of *knowing*.)
- **What *makes* something right or wrong?** (Philosophers call this **ontology** — the
  study of *being*, or what something really is.)

The atheist is right about the first question. Of course you can *know* that lying and
cruelty are wrong without believing in God. The Bible itself says so! God has written
His law on every human heart (Romans 2:15).

But the moral argument is about the **second** question. It's not asking whether
atheists can *know* or *do* good. It's asking what makes "good" really good in the first
place.

> You can drive a car without ever believing that a car company exists. You can use it,
> maintain it and enjoy it. But the car didn't build itself. In the same way, you can live
> a moral life without believing in God — but morality still needs God to exist.

So a gentle reply is: *"You're right that you don't need to believe in God to be good.
But I'm asking a different question: why is anything good at all, if there's no God?"*

## The Law Written on the Heart

This argument connects beautifully with what we learned about the **conscience**.

Romans 2:14–15 explains that even people who've never read the Ten Commandments still
know much of what the Law says. Why? Because *"the work of the law is written on their
hearts."* Their conscience **bears witness** — it speaks up, sometimes accusing them,
sometimes excusing them.

That's why, all over the world, people know that lying, stealing, murder and adultery
are wrong. The Ten Commandments aren't strange foreign rules. They're a written copy of
what God has already stamped on every heart.

> Your conscience is like a smoke alarm God has installed in every human being. You can
> take the batteries out, but it was built to warn you of real danger.

## Where This Argument Leads

Here is the most important part. The moral argument doesn't just prove that there is a
Lawgiver. It shows that there is a law — and **we have all broken it**.

If objective right and wrong exist, then I'm not just "imperfect." I've done things that
are really wrong in God's sight. Romans 3:23 says *"all have sinned and fall short of the
glory of God."* That's not a religious insult; it's what every honest conscience already
admits.

That's why the moral argument is a perfect bridge to the gospel. You start with,
*"Do you think some things are really wrong?"* You end with, *"Have you ever done any of
those things?"* And from there you can walk through the Ten Commandments, the coming Day
of Judgment, and the wonderful news that Jesus paid the penalty for lawbreakers like us.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the three lines** of the moral argument.
2. **Practise the knowing-vs-being distinction** using the car illustration until you can
   explain it in 30 seconds.
3. **Use one question** this week: *"Do you think some things are really wrong — not just
   wrong for you?"* Listen carefully to the answer.
4. **Memorise Romans 2:15** and notice when your own conscience speaks up.
5. **Practise the bridge**: from "some things are really wrong" to "have you ever done
   them?" to the cross.
