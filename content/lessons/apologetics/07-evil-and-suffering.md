objections:
  - q: "If God were good, He'd want to stop evil. If He were powerful, He could. So He can't be both."
    a: >-
      That argument assumes God could have no good reason for allowing evil for a time. But
      we can't know that. Good parents and good doctors sometimes allow pain for a greater
      good. If even we can have such reasons, an all-wise God certainly could, including
      reasons we can't see. Many philosophers today, believers and non-believers alike,
      agree this version of the argument doesn't succeed.
  - q: "Why doesn't God just stop all evil right now?"
    a: >-
      It's worth thinking about what that would mean. Evil doesn't only exist "out there"
      in terrorists and tyrants. It's in every human heart, including yours and mine. If God
      removed all evil tonight, none of us would be left. His patience is actually His mercy.
      He's holding back judgment so people can repent (2 Peter 3:9).
  - q: "What about earthquakes and cancer? Nobody chose those."
    a: >-
      That's the harder question, and it deserves honesty. Christians point to a few things.
      The Bible says the whole creation was affected by humanity's rebellion (Romans 8:20-22).
      A world with stable natural laws, where fire can warm us, is also a world where fire
      can burn. And this broken world is not the final one. None of that removes the pain,
      but it means natural suffering is not evidence that God is absent.
  - q: "I prayed and my mum still died. Where was God?"
    a: >-
      I'm so sorry. That's not a debate question, and I don't want to treat it like one.
      I don't know why God didn't answer the way you hoped. What I do know is that Jesus
      wept at a graveside, and He knows grief from the inside. If you're willing, I'd love to
      hear about your mum.

Someone asks, *"If God is good, why is there so much suffering?"*

The words are the same whether they're spoken by a philosophy student in a seminar or by a
mother beside a hospital bed. But those are **two different questions**, and they need two
very different responses. Learning to tell them apart may be the most important skill in this
lesson.

This is the most common objection to Christianity, and it's an honest one. The Bible doesn't
hide from it. Whole books (Job, Habakkuk, Lamentations) and many psalms wrestle with it.
You don't need to be afraid of it either.

## Two Problems, Not One

**The logical problem of evil** is an intellectual argument. It goes back at least to the
ancient Greek philosopher Epicurus. In simple form:

1. If God were all-good, He would want to stop evil.
2. If God were all-powerful, He could stop evil.
3. Evil exists.
4. So an all-good, all-powerful God doesn't exist.

It sounds airtight, but it carries a hidden assumption: **that God could have no good reason
for allowing evil, even for a while.** Nobody can show that. After the work of Christian
philosopher **Alvin Plantinga** on free will in the 1970s, many philosophers, including many
atheists, agree this strict "contradiction" version does not work. (Some skeptics now argue
a softer version: that evil makes God *less likely*. The answers below help there too.)

**The emotional problem of evil** is the pain of suffering itself. It's the ache of loss, the
anger at injustice, the silence after a prayer. This person doesn't need a syllogism. They need
a friend.

Keep both in mind as we look at **the four C's**, four truths you can remember and use.

## 1. Choice: Real Love Needs Real Freedom

> Imagine a **talking doll** that says "I love you" every time you press its button. It's
> cute, but it doesn't love you. It can't do anything else. Love only means something when
> it's freely given, and that means it could have been withheld.

God made human beings to love Him and each other. That requires **free will**: the real
ability to choose. But a world where people can freely choose good is also a world where
people can choose evil. And much of the world's suffering, including murder, abuse, war,
greed, and neglect, comes straight from human choices.

What about **natural evil**, like earthquakes and disease? That's harder, and it's okay to say
so. Christians point out that the Bible links the brokenness of creation to human rebellion
(Romans 8:20-22). They also note that a world with stable natural laws, which we need in order
to make meaningful choices, is a world where fire can both warm and burn. And they stress that
this is not the world as God made it or as He will remake it.

## 2. Can't-See: God Has Reasons We Can't See

> Picture a **toddler at the doctor's**. Their parent holds them still while a stranger jabs
> them with a needle. From the child's point of view, it's a betrayal. The child simply can't
> understand the reason. The gap between a toddler and a parent is tiny compared with the gap
> between us and God.

"I can't see a reason" is not the same as "there is no reason." Joseph's story shows this.
Sold by his brothers, falsely accused, and forgotten in prison, he later saw God had used all
of it to save many lives (Genesis 50:20). But he lived through years of darkness before he
understood.

Job never learned why he suffered. When God finally spoke, He didn't give Job an
explanation. He gave Job **Himself**. Sometimes that's what we get too, and it turns out to be
enough.

Scripture also asks us to weigh suffering against **eternity**. Paul, who was beaten, shipwrecked,
and imprisoned, called his troubles "light momentary affliction" compared with the glory to
come (2 Corinthians 4:17). He wasn't saying pain is small. He was saying eternity is
unimaginably large. A lifetime of sorrow is real, but it is not the whole story.

## 3. Cross: God Entered Our Suffering

Other worldviews offer explanations of suffering. Christianity offers a God who **suffered**.

Jesus wept at His friend Lazarus's tomb, even though He knew He was about to raise him (John
11:35). Isaiah called Him "a man of sorrows" (Isaiah 53:3). He was betrayed, tortured, and
executed, the worst injustice in history. Yet God turned that very evil into the greatest good
the world has ever known: the payment for sin and the defeat of death (Acts 2:23-24).

So when someone asks, *"Where is God in my suffering?"*, the Christian can point to the cross
and say: **He has been there. He knows.** And because Jesus rose, suffering is not the end.
The story finishes with God wiping away every tear (Revelation 21:4).

## 4. Crooked Line: Evil Points Back to God

Here's a surprising turn. When we call something truly **evil**, and not just unpleasant, we
are measuring it against a standard of **good**. C.S. Lewis noticed this when he was an
atheist: he couldn't call the universe unjust without some idea of justice to compare it with.

If there is no God, nature is just matter in motion. A tsunami isn't evil. It's water. A
cruel act is simply something we dislike. But almost nobody really believes that. We know
that torturing children is **really wrong**, not just unpopular. That moral knowledge fits
far better with a good God than with a universe that doesn't care. (For more, see *Morality
Points to a Moral Lawgiver*.)

Say this gently. It's not a gotcha. It doesn't make anyone's pain hurt less. But it shows
that the problem of evil is actually a problem **for everyone**, and that the Christian has
somewhere to stand.

And there's one more uncomfortable truth. The line between good and evil doesn't run between
"good people" and "bad people." It runs **through every human heart**. If we ask God to remove
all evil today, we're asking Him to remove us. His delay is mercy: He is patient, "not wishing
that any should perish" (2 Peter 3:9). The cross is how He deals with evil without destroying
the evildoer. He took the judgment Himself, so that people like us could be forgiven.

## Answer the Person, Not Just the Question

When Job lost everything, his friends came and sat with him in silence for **seven days** (Job
2:13). That was the best thing they did. Things went wrong when they started talking and
confidently explaining why he was suffering.

When someone is hurting:

1. **Listen first.** Ask, *"What happened?"* or *"Tell me about them."*
2. **Don't rush to a verse.** Romans 8:28 is true, but thrown at a grieving person it can feel
   like a slap. Earn the right to share it.
3. **Grieve honestly.** The Bible gives us laments like Psalm 13 and Psalm 88. It's okay to say,
   *"This is terrible."*
4. **Be practically present.** Meals, messages, and showing up weeks later when others have
   stopped.
5. **Point to Jesus when they're ready:** the God who wept, suffered, and rose.

1. **Memorise the four C's:** Choice, Can't-see, Cross, Crooked line.
2. **Memorise Genesis 50:20** with the flashcards.
3. **Read Job 38–42** this week and notice what God gives Job instead of an explanation.
4. **Think of one person who is hurting.** Send them a message this week. Don't try to fix
   anything. Just let them know you care.
5. **Practise a 30-second answer** to "Why does God allow evil?" that ends at the cross.
