      Jewish interpreters also read this passage as describing the Messiah.

      something yet isn't the same as showing it never existed. Most ancient sites have

Imagine you open a time capsule buried hundreds of years ago. Inside is a letter describing
a person who hasn't been born yet: the small town they'll be born in, the family they'll come
from, how they'll be treated, and how they'll die. Then you realise it describes someone you
know.

You'd want to know who wrote that letter.

Christians claim the Bible contains something like that about Jesus. In this lesson we'll
look at three kinds of evidence that the Bible is more than an ordinary book. To remember
them, think of **the three P's**:

1. **Prophecy:** a portrait of Jesus painted centuries in advance.
2. **Pickaxe:** archaeology that keeps confirming the Bible's history.
3. **Plotline:** one story told through 66 books by about 40 authors.

None of these is a knockdown "proof." Together, though, they make a strong case that the
Bible deserves a serious hearing.

## 1. Prophecy: A Portrait Painted in Advance

A **prophecy** here means a message from God that includes a prediction about the future.
The Old Testament was finished centuries before Jesus, yet it describes a coming
**Messiah** (literally "anointed one," God's promised King and Rescuer).

> Think of a **police sketch**. One detail ("male") fits millions of people. Add "born in
> Bethlehem," "descended from King David," "rejected by his own people," "dies for others,"
> "buried with the rich," and "lives again after death," and the lineup shrinks to one face.

Here are the details worth memorising:

- **Born in Bethlehem**, a tiny village (Micah 5:2).
- **From King David's family line** (Isaiah 11:1; Jeremiah 23:5).
- **Rejected, and suffering for other people's sins** (Isaiah 53:3-6).
- **Killed alongside criminals but buried with the rich** (Isaiah 53:9, 12).
- **Mocked, with his clothes divided by casting lots** (Psalm 22:7-8, 18).
- **Alive after death**: "he shall prolong his days" (Isaiah 53:10).

Jesus quoted the opening line of Psalm 22 from the cross: "My God, my God, why have you
forsaken me?" He was pointing His hearers to the whole psalm.

## Handling the Dating Honestly

A skeptic will reasonably ask: *"How do we know these weren't written after the fact?"* Here
is how to answer without overstating anything.

**Isaiah.** Traditionally, the whole book is credited to the prophet Isaiah in the 700s BC.
Many critical scholars instead think chapters 40–66 were written later, around the time of the
Babylonian exile (500s BC). Christians hold different views on this. But here's the key point:
**it doesn't matter for the argument.** Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, found in 1947, is the
**Great Isaiah Scroll**, a complete copy of Isaiah made roughly in the second century BC. On any
dating, Isaiah 53 existed well before Jesus was born.

**Psalm 22.** This psalm is traditionally credited to David, around 1000 BC, long before
crucifixion was invented. Be aware that one line, "they have pierced my hands and feet"
(22:16), is debated. Most Hebrew manuscripts read something like "like a lion," while the
ancient Greek translation and at least one Dead Sea fragment support a reading like
"pierced." Don't build your case on that one word. The psalm as a whole, with its mockery,
thirst, and gambling for clothes, is striking enough.

**Patterns, not only predictions.** Some New Testament "fulfilments" are not predictions at
all. Matthew 2:15 applies Hosea 11:1 ("Out of Egypt I called my son") to Jesus, but Hosea was
describing Israel's exodus. This is **typology**, a pattern in Israel's story that Jesus fills
out and completes. Knowing the difference keeps you honest, and it helps you avoid claiming
more than the text says.

**Could Jesus have staged it?** Some things He clearly did on purpose. Riding a donkey into
Jerusalem (Zechariah 9:9) was a public claim to be the King. But no one picks their
birthplace, their ancestors, how their executioners kill them, or what soldiers do with their
clothes.

## 2. The Pickaxe: What Archaeology Shows

**Archaeology** is the study of the past through physical remains such as buildings,
inscriptions, coins, and pottery. It can't test miracles, but it can test whether the Bible
gets real people, places, and customs right.

> Archaeology confirms **the stage, not the play**. It can show the theatre was real, the
> actors really lived, and the street names are correct. It can't dig up a resurrection or
> a forgiven sin. Still, a book that keeps getting the stage right earns a hearing on the play.

Four finds to know:

1. **The Pilate Stone (1961).** A limestone block found at Caesarea Maritima naming "Pontius
   Pilate, Prefect of Judea," the Roman governor who sentenced Jesus.
2. **The Tel Dan Stele (1993).** An Aramaic inscription from the 800s BC, set up by an enemy
   king, that refers to the "House of David." Before this, some scholars treated David as a
   legend. It is the earliest known reference to David outside the Bible.
3. **The Pool of Siloam (2004).** Workers repairing a sewer pipe in Jerusalem uncovered steps
   of a large pool from the time of Jesus. It's where Jesus sent a blind man to wash (John 9:7).
4. **The Hittites.** The Old Testament mentions them often (Genesis 23; Uriah the Hittite in
   2 Samuel 11). In the 1800s they were almost unknown outside the Bible, and some scholars
   doubted they had been a major people. Then excavations in Turkey in the early 1900s
   uncovered their capital, Hattusa, and thousands of their clay tablets.

**Be honest about the limits.** Some questions are still debated, even among believing
archaeologists. The date and details of the Exodus and the conquest of Jericho are examples.
Don't say "archaeology has proven the Bible." Say something truer: *where the Bible can be
checked, it has held up well, sometimes after experts had doubted it.*

## 3. The Plotline: One Story in 66 Books

The Protestant Bible has **66 books** (Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include some additional
books). They were written:

- by about **40 authors**, including kings, shepherds, fishermen, a tax collector, and a doctor;
- over roughly **1,500 years**;
- in **three languages** (Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek);
- in many genres, including history, law, poetry, prophecy, letters, and visions.

Yet it tells **one story**, which you can memorise in four words:

1. **Creation:** God makes a good world.
2. **Fall:** humans rebel, and sin and death enter.
3. **Redemption:** God rescues through His Son.
4. **Restoration:** He will one day make all things new.

> Imagine a **chain novel** written by forty people across fifteen centuries. Most never met,
> and none saw the ending. Yet the first chapter plants a promise (Genesis 3:15), the middle
> chapters keep developing it (the Passover lamb, the sacrifices, Isaiah's suffering servant),
> and the last chapter pays it off: John calls Jesus "the Lamb of God" (John 1:29), and
> Revelation 5 shows the Lamb on the throne.

Unity alone doesn't prove the Bible is from God, and it doesn't mean every passage is easy.
But it is exactly what you'd expect if one Author was guiding many writers (2 Peter 1:21).

## Where the Three P's Lead

Put the strands together and a picture emerges: a book grounded in real history, with one
storyline, centred on a Person described before He came.

And notice *what* the prophecies describe. It isn't a conquering celebrity. It's a
**substitute**: "he was pierced for our transgressions." That's where every conversation
about the Bible's reliability should end up. The evidence matters because of the Person it
points to, and the reason He was pierced is our sin. If the Bible is right about Bethlehem,
Pilate, and Siloam, it's worth listening to when it says Jesus died so that we could be
forgiven.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the three P's** (Prophecy, Pickaxe, Plotline) and one example of each.
2. **Learn three prophecies by reference:** Micah 5:2, Isaiah 53, Psalm 22.
3. **Learn the four finds** in one sentence each. Say them out loud until they come easily.
4. **Read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 this week**, then read it to a friend and ask, "Who do you think
   this is about?" It's the same question the Ethiopian official asked in Acts 8:34.
5. **Practise the four-word plotline** (Creation, Fall, Redemption, Restoration) until you can
   explain it in 30 seconds.
