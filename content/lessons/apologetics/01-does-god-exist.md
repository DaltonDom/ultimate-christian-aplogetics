  - q: "Science will eventually explain the beginning without God."
    a: >-
      Science is brilliant at explaining how things work *inside* the universe. But the
      beginning of the universe is the beginning of nature itself — space, time, matter
      and energy. Whatever caused nature can't be part of nature. So this isn't a gap
      science hasn't filled yet; it's a question science by its nature points beyond. And
      notice that "science will explain it someday" is a statement of faith, not evidence.

      properties, no laws, no potential. It can't do anything, because there's nothing
      there to do it. If things could pop into being from literal nothing, why don't we
      see bicycles and elephants appearing in our living rooms?

      immaterial, unimaginably powerful and personal — which rules out atheism and a lot
      of other options. From there, we can look at who God has revealed Himself to be,

Imagine you're hiking deep in a forest and you come across a small wooden cabin. It has
a door, a window, a chimney, and a neat stack of firewood by the step. You've never seen
the person who built it. You'll probably never meet them.

But would you ever think, *"This cabin just happened"*? Of course not. The cabin tells
you there was a builder, even though the builder is nowhere in sight.

The Bible makes the same point in one sentence: *every house is built by someone, but
the builder of all things is God* (Hebrews 3:4). That simple intuition — **a building
needs a builder, a painting needs a painter** — is where the case for God begins. In this
lesson we'll turn that intuition into a clear argument you can memorise and use.

## The Common-Sense Starting Point

Most people, in most cultures, throughout history have believed in some kind of God.
That's not because they were foolish. It's because they looked at the world and saw
what the Bible says they would see.

- **Psalm 19:1** says the sky *"proclaims his handiwork."* Every sunrise is a signature.
- **Romans 1:20** says God's *"eternal power and divine nature"* are *"clearly perceived"*
  in the things He has made — so clearly that people are *"without excuse."*

Notice what Paul is claiming. He isn't saying that you need a university degree to know
God exists. He's saying the evidence is **built into reality** and available to
everyone. The farmer, the child and the physicist all look at the same sky.

> A painting proves there was a painter. You don't need to meet the artist, know their
> name, or see them holding the brush. The painting itself is the evidence.

But some people will push back: *"That's just a feeling. Can you give me an actual
reason?"* Yes, you can. That's where the next argument comes in.

## The Kalam Argument in Plain Words

The **kalam cosmological argument** sounds complicated, but it's simple. *Kalam* is an
Arabic word used by medieval thinkers who developed this reasoning. *Cosmological* just
means "about the cosmos" — the universe. In our day it has been revived and popularised
by the Christian philosopher **William Lane Craig**.

Here it is in three lines. Memorise them:

1. **Whatever begins to exist has a cause.**
2. **The universe began to exist.**
3. **Therefore, the universe has a cause.**

That's it. If the first two lines are true, the third line follows automatically — just
like "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; so Socrates is mortal." So the only
question is: are lines 1 and 2 true?

### Line 1: Whatever begins to exist has a cause

This is one of the most obvious truths we know. Things don't just pop into being with
no cause at all. If you heard a loud bang in the kitchen, you would never say, *"Oh,
nothing caused that."* Science itself depends on this principle — scientists search for
causes precisely because they assume everything that happens has one.

### Line 2: The universe began to exist

For most of history, many thinkers assumed the universe was eternal. But two lines of
evidence point the other way.

- **Science:** In the twentieth century, astronomers discovered that the universe is
  **expanding**. Run the film backwards and everything gets closer and closer together
  until you reach a starting point. That's what scientists call the **Big Bang**. There
  is still plenty of debate about the very earliest moments, and we shouldn't pretend
  otherwise — but the mainstream view in cosmology is that our universe had a beginning.
- **Logic:** Try to imagine an infinite number of days *before* today. If the past
  really went back forever, you would have to get through an infinite number of days to
  arrive at today. But you can never finish counting through infinity. So today would
  never arrive. Yet here we are.

> Imagine someone tells you, *"I've just finished counting down from infinity: …three,
> two, one, zero!"* You'd ask, *"When did you start?"* And the answer would be, *"I never
> started."* That's the problem with an infinite past. You can't finish a journey that
> never began.

### Line 3: So the universe has a cause

If lines 1 and 2 are true, the conclusion is unavoidable. The universe didn't just
happen. Something caused it.

## What Kind of Cause?

Here's where it gets exciting. Think about what the cause of the universe must be like.
The universe includes all **time**, **space** and **matter**. So its cause must be:

- **Timeless** — because it created time.
- **Spaceless and immaterial** — because it created space and matter.
- **Unimaginably powerful** — because it created everything from nothing.
- **Personal** — because an impersonal, frozen state of affairs would never "decide" to
  start a universe. Only a **mind** with a will can freely choose to act.

Put that list together and read it back slowly: an eternal, immaterial, all-powerful,
personal Creator. That sounds a lot like the opening line of the Bible: *"In the
beginning, God created the heavens and the earth"* (Genesis 1:1).

It's honest to admit that this argument doesn't prove everything Christians believe. It
doesn't tell you that God became a man in Jesus. But it clears away the idea that the
universe is all there is, and it points straight at a Creator.

## "Who Made God?" and "Something from Nothing"

You will hear these two replies more than any others. Be ready for both.

**"Who made God?"** Notice that line 1 says *whatever begins to exist* has a cause. It
doesn't say *everything* has a cause. God, by definition, never began. He is **eternal**
— He has always existed. So He doesn't need a cause.

This isn't a special exception made up for God. Everyone believes **something** is
eternal, because out of nothing, nothing comes. The atheist once said the eternal thing
was the universe. But the evidence shows the universe had a beginning. So the eternal
thing must be something beyond it.

> Think of a long line of train carriages. Each carriage is pulled by the one in front.
> Adding more carriages — even a million — never explains the movement. Somewhere, there
> must be an **engine** that isn't pulled by anything else. God is the engine.

**"The universe came from nothing."** Ask gently, *"What do you mean by nothing?"* Some
popular writers use "nothing" to mean empty space or a **quantum vacuum** — a sea of
energy governed by physical laws. But that's not nothing. That's *something*. Real
**nothing** means no space, no energy, no laws, no potential — not anything at all. And
nothing has no power to do anything. If things could pop into existence from literal
nothing, we'd have no reason to be surprised if a horse appeared in the living room.

## Where This Leads

Knowing that God exists is a huge step. But the Bible says it isn't enough. Even the
demons believe God exists — and shudder (James 2:19). Romans 1 goes on to say that
people know God is there but choose not to honour Him. That's the real problem: not a
lack of evidence, but a heart that runs from its Maker.

So don't treat the kalam argument as the finish line. Use it as a **door**. Once someone
admits there is a Creator, the natural next question is: *"If God made you, do you think
He cares how you live? Are you ready to meet Him?"* That opens the way to the Law, the
conscience, and the cross. When Paul spoke in Athens (Acts 17), he went from creation to
the Creator, then straight to repentance and the resurrection. That's our model.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the three lines** of the kalam argument until you can say them without
   thinking.
2. **Learn the four features** of the cause: timeless, spaceless, powerful, personal.
3. **Practise the train-carriage illustration** out loud as your answer to "Who made
   God?"
4. **Memorise Hebrews 3:4** with the flashcards, and use the cabin illustration with a
   friend this week.
5. **Plan your bridge.** Write one sentence you could use to move from "God exists" to
   "Are you ready to meet Him?"
