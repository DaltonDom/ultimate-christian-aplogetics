*"I'm a science person. I don't really do religion."*

You'll hear this a lot, especially from students and young adults. Behind it is a story many
people have absorbed without thinking: **science and faith are enemies, and science is
winning.**

This lesson will help you gently untangle that story. We'll look at three distinctions that
clear up most of the confusion, and then at how to keep the conversation on what matters most.

## 1. Science and Christianity Are Not Enemies

> Ask, *"Why is the kettle boiling?"* One answer: *"Because heat is making the water
> molecules move faster until they turn to steam."* Another answer: *"Because I want a cup of
> tea."* Both are true. They answer **different kinds of questions**. One is about
> **mechanism** (how), the other about **agency and purpose** (who and why).

Science is superb at "how" questions. But finding the mechanism never rules out the agent. If
you understand how a car engine works, you haven't shown that there's no engineer.

History backs this up. Many founders of modern science were believers:

- **Johannes Kepler**, who discovered the laws of planetary motion, was a devout Lutheran.
- **Robert Boyle**, a founder of modern chemistry, was a committed Christian who wrote on theology.
- **Isaac Newton** was deeply religious, though unorthodox on the Trinity.
- **Michael Faraday** and **James Clerk Maxwell**, pioneers of electricity and magnetism, were
  devout Christians.
- **Gregor Mendel**, the father of genetics, was an Augustinian friar.
- **Georges Lemaître**, who first proposed what became the Big Bang theory, was a Catholic
  priest.

Why were so many believers? Because they expected the universe to be **orderly and
understandable**, since it was made by a rational God. They saw science as exploring His
handiwork (Proverbs 25:2). Historians of science today largely reject the "warfare" story,
which was popularised in the late 1800s.

That story isn't only about the past. Many working scientists today are Christians, in
physics, medicine, genetics, and astronomy. When someone says, *"You can't be a scientist and
a Christian,"* the simplest reply is that thousands of people are both. The real tension is
not between science and God. It is between two **worldviews** that both try to interpret what
science finds. That brings us to the second distinction.

## 2. Method vs Worldview

Two terms clear up a great deal:

- **Methodological naturalism** is science's working method. When studying nature, look for
  natural causes. This is sensible, and Christian scientists use it every day.
- **Philosophical naturalism** is a worldview. It is the belief that nature is **all there
  is**, with no God, no soul, and nothing beyond the physical.

The first does not prove the second.

> The astronomer **Arthur Eddington** told a parable about a man studying sea life with a net
> whose holes were two inches (5 cm) wide. After many catches he concluded: *"No sea creature
> is smaller than two inches."* His net was simply unable to catch small fish. Science's net is built to
> catch natural causes. That doesn't mean nothing else exists.

So when someone says "Science has disproved God," you can ask kindly: *"Which experiment
showed that?"* Usually the claim is really philosophical naturalism dressed up in a lab coat.

## 3. Miracles and David Hume

A **miracle** is an event God brings about that nature on its own would not produce.

Does that "break" the laws of nature? Not really. **Laws of nature** describe what happens
when nothing outside the system interferes. C.S. Lewis made a similar point in his book *Miracles*.
If you drop a ball, gravity says it will hit the floor. If I catch it, I haven't broken
gravity. I've added a new cause. A miracle is God adding a new cause to His own creation.

The most famous argument against miracles comes from the Scottish philosopher **David Hume**
(1748). In simple terms:

1. Laws of nature are established by our **uniform experience**.
2. A miracle goes against that experience.
3. So the evidence against a miracle will always be greater than any evidence for it.

Here are two problems with it:

- **It's circular.** Experience is only "uniform" against miracles if you've already decided
  every report of a miracle is false. That assumes the very thing being argued.
- **Rare isn't the same as false.** Hume himself discussed an Indian prince who refused to
  believe travellers' reports that water turns solid in cold countries. Hume thought the
  prince reasoned sensibly from his own "uniform experience." Yet the prince was wrong. Water
  really does freeze. Unusual events need good evidence, not a closed door.

If God exists, miracles are possible. The honest question is simply: **is there good evidence
that one happened?** For the central miracle of Christianity, see *The Resurrection of Jesus*.
(And yes, Christians should be discerning too. Not every claimed miracle is genuine.)

## 4. Unpacking "Evolution"

**"Evolution" is a suitcase word.** Before you agree or disagree, unpack it. It can mean:

1. **Change over time within populations.** Bacteria develop antibiotic resistance, and
   finches' beaks vary with the food supply. This is observed and uncontroversial.
   Sometimes called *microevolution*.
2. **Common descent.** All living things descend from a common ancestor through gradual change.
   Sometimes called *macroevolution*. **Christians genuinely disagree about this.**
3. **Evolutionism.** Life is the product of a purposeless, unguided process, so God is
   unnecessary. That is not a scientific finding. It's philosophical naturalism again, and
   **all Christians reject it.**

On meaning 2, the debate is real. Intelligent design advocates, such as biochemist Michael
Behe and philosopher of science Stephen Meyer, argue that some features of life (like the
information in DNA) point to a designer and are not well explained by unguided processes.
Most mainstream biologists disagree with them. Evolutionary creationists, like geneticist
Francis Collins, accept common descent and see it as the way God chose to create. See the
**Where Christians Differ** section for the three main views.

Whatever your view, the bigger questions remain untouched by biology. Why is there a universe
at all? Why are its laws finely tuned for life? (See *Does God Exist?* and *Design and
Fine-Tuning*.)

## Keep the Main Thing the Main Thing

> Think of a house. Some walls you can move without much trouble. One wall holds up the roof.
> In Christianity, the **load-bearing wall** is the resurrection of Jesus.

Paul didn't say "if the earth is old, your faith is futile." He said, *"if Christ has not been
raised."* That's where the weight rests.

So don't let a conversation stall in Genesis 1. You can say: *"Christians disagree about that,
and it's worth discussing. But even if you're right about evolution, it can't tell you what
happened to Jesus' body. Can we look at that?"*

The resurrection matters because of what it means. Jesus is Lord, His death paid for sin, and
a day of judgment is coming (Acts 17:31). The God who made the heavens that "declare His
glory" also came into His creation to rescue it. That's the conversation worth steering
toward.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the three distinctions:** How vs who (kettle), method vs worldview (Eddington's
   net), and the three meanings of evolution (suitcase).
2. **Memorise Psalm 19:1** with the flashcards.
3. **Learn three believing scientists**, such as Kepler, Faraday, and Lemaître, and what each
   discovered.
4. **Practise the pivot:** *"Even if you're right about evolution, what happened to Jesus'
   body?"* Say it out loud until it feels natural.
5. **Look up at the night sky** this week and pray Psalm 19:1 back to God. Let science lead
   you to worship.
