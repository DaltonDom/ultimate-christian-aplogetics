  - q: "Of course the universe is right for life — if it weren't, we wouldn't be here to notice."
    a: >-
      That's true, but it doesn't explain anything. Imagine you're in front of a firing
      squad of fifty trained marksmen. They all fire — and all miss. You could say, "Well,
      if they hadn't missed, I wouldn't be here to wonder about it." True! But you'd still
      rightly ask, "Why did they all miss?" and suspect it was planned. Being alive to
      notice doesn't make the fine-tuning any less surprising. (This illustration comes
      from the philosopher John Leslie.)
  - q: "Maybe there are infinite universes, and we're just in the lucky one."
    a: >-
      That's the multiverse idea, and it's worth taking seriously. But there's no direct
      evidence for other universes — we can't observe them, even in principle. Many
      proposals also need a "universe generator" that would itself have to be finely
      tuned. And if you accept infinite universes to explain away design, you could
      explain away anything — a poker player dealt twenty royal flushes in a row could
      say, "There are infinite card games somewhere." Nobody would buy it at the table.
  - q: "If life was designed, why is there so much that seems badly designed or cruel?"
    a: >-
      A dented car was still designed. The Bible says the world was made good but has been
      damaged by sin (Genesis 3; Romans 8:20-22). So we'd expect to see both brilliant
      design *and* brokenness — which is exactly what we see. The question of suffering is
      real, and we'll deal with it in its own lesson, but it doesn't erase the evidence of
      a designer.
  - q: "Isn't this just a 'God of the gaps' argument?"
    a: >-
      A "God of the gaps" argument says, "We don't know how this happened, so God did it."
      Design arguments work differently: they're based on what we *do* know. We know from
      everyday experience that finely-tuned settings and coded messages come from minds.
      So we're reasoning from our knowledge, not our ignorance.

Imagine you're walking along a beach. The tide has just gone out, and the sand is
smooth. Then you see it, written in big letters: **"SAM LOVES JESS."**

You'd never think, *"Wow, look what the waves did."* Waves can make ripples, and wind can
make patterns. But a **message** — letters arranged in a specific order to carry a
meaning — tells you instantly that someone was here. A mind was at work.

That simple instinct is at the heart of this lesson. In the last lesson we saw that the
universe had a beginning, so it needs a cause. Now we'll look at *how* the universe and
life are put together — and see that they carry the fingerprints of a Designer.

## The Universe's Control Panel

Picture a control panel with dozens of dials. Each dial controls one setting of the
universe: the strength of **gravity**, the force that holds **atoms** together, the rate
at which space **expands**, the **mass** of tiny particles, and so on. Scientists call
these settings the **constants** and **quantities** of nature.

Here's the astonishing part. Physicists — including many who don't believe in God —
have found that many of these dials must be set within an extremely narrow range for
life to be possible at all. This is called **fine-tuning**.

- If **gravity** were a bit stronger or weaker, stars either wouldn't form properly or
  would burn out too quickly to support life.
- If the **strong nuclear force** (the "glue" in the centre of atoms) were slightly
  different, you wouldn't get the variety of elements, like carbon and oxygen, that life
  needs.
- The **rate of cosmic expansion** seems to be tuned so precisely that some estimates put
  it at many dozens of decimal places. Too fast, and matter would scatter before galaxies
  could form; too slow, and everything would collapse back.

Scientists still debate the exact numbers, and we shouldn't claim more precision than the
evidence gives. But the overall picture is widely acknowledged: **a universe that allows
life is extraordinarily special.**

> Imagine throwing a dart from one side of a stadium and hitting a target the size of a
> postage stamp on the other side. Now imagine doing it again, and again, for dial after
> dial. At some point you stop saying "lucky shot" and start saying "expert thrower."

## The Fine-Tuning Argument in Three Lines

Philosophers like William Lane Craig lay out the argument like this. Memorise it:

1. **The fine-tuning of the universe is due to physical necessity, chance, or design.**
2. **It is not due to physical necessity or chance.**
3. **Therefore, it is due to design.**

Why not the first two?

- **Physical necessity** means "the dials *had* to be set this way — there was no other
  option." But there's no known reason the constants couldn't have been different. They
  seem to be independent settings, not forced by any deeper law.
- **Chance** means "we just got lucky." But the odds are so low that relying on luck is
  like a lottery ticket winning every draw for a year. At some point, "luck" stops being
  a reasonable explanation.

That leaves **design**. And that fits exactly what the Bible says: God *"formed the earth
... to be inhabited"* (Isaiah 45:18), and in Christ *"all things hold together"*
(Colossians 1:17).

## A Message Needs a Mind: The Information in DNA

Now zoom in from the universe to a single cell in your body. Inside is **DNA**, a long
molecule that works like an instruction manual for building and running a living thing.

DNA uses a four-letter chemical "alphabet" — usually written as **A, C, G and T**. The
order of these letters matters, just like the order of letters in a sentence. The cell
**reads** the sequence and uses it to build **proteins**, the tiny machines that do the
work of life. Change the order, and you change the meaning — or get nonsense.

This is called **information**: a specific arrangement of symbols that carries
instructions. The human genome contains roughly three billion of these letters.

Here's the argument, popularised by thinkers such as Stephen Meyer:

1. **DNA contains coded information.**
2. **In all our experience, coded information comes from a mind.**
3. **Therefore, DNA points to a mind.**

Think about it: every book, computer program and street sign you've ever seen was
written by someone. We have never observed a code arise from purely unguided chemistry.
So when we find the most sophisticated code we know of, inside every living cell,
the most reasonable explanation is an intelligent author.

> If "SAM LOVES JESS" in the sand points to a writer, what about three billion letters of
> precise instructions?

To be fair, scientists who study the **origin of life** are working hard on how the
first self-copying molecules might have formed without a designer, and some of their
experiments are genuinely clever. You don't need to mock that work. But it's honest to
say that, so far, no one has shown how an unguided process could produce the kind of
specific, functional information found in even the simplest living cell. And notice
something about those experiments: every one of them is carefully planned and run by an
intelligent scientist.

Notice this is not a "God of the gaps" argument. We're not saying, "We don't know, so
God." We're reasoning from what we *do* know: messages come from minds.

## The Multiverse Reply

The most common scientific response to fine-tuning is the **multiverse** — the idea
that there might be a vast (maybe infinite) number of universes, each with different
settings. If so, one of them was bound to get lucky, and we're in it.

This is a serious proposal, and some brilliant scientists take it seriously. But notice
a few things:

- **There's no direct evidence for it.** Other universes, by definition, can't be
  observed from ours. It's an idea, not a discovery.
- **It pushes the question back.** Many versions need a "universe-making machine," and
  that machine would itself need to be finely tuned.
- **It can explain away anything.** If you accept infinite universes to avoid design,
  you could explain *any* improbable event the same way. A card player dealt twenty royal
  flushes in a row could say, "Somewhere there are infinite card games." Nobody at the
  table would believe him.

It's fair to point out that a Designer is a simpler explanation than an infinite number
of unseen universes.

## From Designer to Saviour

The design argument shows that we are not accidents. We are, as Psalm 139:14 says,
**fearfully and wonderfully made**. That's good news — but it also raises a serious
question. If Someone designed you, you belong to Him. You were made *for* a purpose,
and you're accountable to the One who made you (Colossians 1:16).

That's the natural bridge to the gospel. A designed world is one with a Designer who has
standards. And every one of us has fallen short of them. The same Christ through whom
all things were made came into His own world to rescue the people He made.
