Imagine your great-grandmother had a famous chocolate cake recipe. She wrote it on a
card, and before the card was lost, twenty relatives copied it by hand. Over the years,
their children and grandchildren copied those copies too.

Now there are a hundred versions of the recipe floating around the family. A few say
"tspn" instead of "teaspoon." One has "2 eggs" where everyone else says "3." Someone
accidentally wrote the sugar line twice.

Could you work out Great-Grandma's original? **Absolutely.** You'd lay all the copies
side by side, notice where one copy slipped, and see what the others agree on. The more
copies you have, the **easier** it gets.

That's basically how scholars know what the Bible originally said. In this lesson you'll
learn the key facts — and how to answer the common objection that the Bible has been
changed beyond recognition.

## A Few Terms First

- **Manuscript** — a handwritten copy (from Latin words meaning "written by hand").
  Before the printing press (around 1450), every Bible was copied by hand.
- **Autograph** — the original document written by the author. We don't have the
  autographs of the Bible — but we don't have them for *any* ancient book.
- **Textual criticism** — the careful science of comparing manuscripts to work out the
  original wording. (*Criticism* here means "careful study," not "attacking.")
- **Variant** — any place where manuscripts differ, even by one letter.
- **Canon** — the list of books recognised as Scripture. The word originally meant a
  measuring rod.

## The Mountain of Evidence

When historians ask, *"Can we trust that we know what an ancient author wrote?"* they
look at two things: **how many** copies we have, and **how early** they are.

Here's how the New Testament compares, using round, honest numbers:

- **New Testament:** more than **5,000** Greek manuscripts (from small fragments to
  complete Bibles), plus around **10,000** in Latin and thousands more in other early
  languages like Syriac and Coptic. The earliest small fragments may date to within
  roughly a century of the originals, and some substantial papyri to around AD 200.
  Complete New Testaments survive from the 300s.
- **Julius Caesar's *Gallic War*:** a few hundred manuscripts, the earliest good ones
  copied roughly nine centuries after Caesar wrote.
- **Tacitus's *Annals*** (a major Roman history): the first six books depend largely on a
  **single** manuscript copied about eight centuries later.
- **Homer's *Iliad*** — the most famous book of the ancient world — is very well
  preserved for an ancient work, with somewhere under 2,000 manuscripts. That's
  impressive, but still well short of the New Testament's total.

Historians trust what they know of Caesar and Tacitus. By that same standard, the New
Testament is in a class of its own. If we doubt the New Testament text, we'd have to
throw out most of ancient history first.

> The problem for New Testament scholars isn't too little evidence. It's an
> embarrassment of riches.

## Why It's Not a Game of Telephone

You've probably heard the objection: *"The Bible is like a game of telephone. It's been
passed on so many times, the message must be garbled."*

In telephone, one person whispers to the next in a single line. Nobody writes anything
down, nobody can check, and only the last version survives. Of course it gets garbled.

The Bible's transmission was completely different:

- **Many lines, not one.** Copies spread quickly to cities across the Roman world —
  Egypt, Syria, Italy, Asia Minor. Each region's copies form an independent "family."
- **Written, not whispered.** Scribes copied from a physical text in front of them.
- **We have the early copies.** We don't just have the last person in the line. We have
  thousands of copies from across the centuries, including very early ones, so we can
  compare them.

And modern Bibles aren't "translations of translations." Translators go back to the
Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek manuscripts directly.

## Textual Variants, Honestly Explained

Here's something skeptics sometimes use to shock people: there are **hundreds of
thousands** of variants among New Testament manuscripts. Some even say there are more
variants than words in the New Testament.

That's true — and it's important to be honest about it. But here's what that number
means. Every difference in every manuscript counts as a variant. If one word is spelled
differently in 2,000 manuscripts, that can count as 2,000 variants. The more manuscripts
you have, the more variants you'll count. **A large number of variants is a sign of a
huge amount of evidence, not a corrupted text.**

Scholars sort variants into categories:

1. **Spelling and slips** — by far the most common. Like "tspn" for "teaspoon."
2. **Word order and small words** — Greek can shuffle word order without changing the
   meaning, and scribes sometimes added or dropped a "the."
3. **Meaningful but not likely original** — a change in one late manuscript that has no
   real chance of being what the author wrote.
4. **Meaningful and possibly original** — a small fraction. For example, in Romans 5:1,
   manuscripts differ over "we *have* peace" or "let us *have* peace" — a one-letter
   difference in Greek. Both are true, biblical ideas.

A few longer passages are debated, like the ending of Mark (16:9–20) and the story of the
woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11). Honest Christian scholars discuss these
openly, and most modern Bibles, including the ESV, mark them with a note. You don't need
to hide them — pointing them out shows you have nothing to hide.

Here's the bottom line: **no core Christian belief** — the deity of Christ, His death and
resurrection, salvation by grace through faith — depends on a doubtful variant. Scholars
across the spectrum, including skeptical ones, agree that the vast majority of variants
are minor.

## The Dead Sea Scrolls: A Thousand-Year Test

What about the Old Testament? Before 1947, the oldest complete Hebrew copies of books
like Isaiah were from around **AD 900–1000** — the medieval Masoretic manuscripts. Some
people wondered how much the text had changed over the previous thousand years or more.

Then, in 1947, a Bedouin shepherd found clay jars in a cave near **Qumran**, by the Dead
Sea. Inside were ancient scrolls. Over the following years, researchers found hundreds
of manuscripts, including portions of nearly every Old Testament book.

The most famous is the **Great Isaiah Scroll**, a complete copy of Isaiah usually dated
to the second century BC — roughly **a thousand years older** than the previous oldest
complete copies. When scholars compared them, the two were overwhelmingly the same. Most
differences were spelling and small details, with no change to the book's message.

> It was like finding Great-Grandma's recipe card in an old tin — and discovering that the
> family had been copying it faithfully all along.

## How the Canon Came Together (in Brief)

**The Old Testament:** Jesus spoke of the Scriptures as "the Law of Moses and the Prophets
and the Psalms" (Luke 24:44) — the three-part Hebrew Bible, which contains the same
content as the 39 books in Protestant Bibles. (Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include some
additional books; we'll look at that in the lesson on Roman Catholics.)

**The New Testament:** The church didn't *create* the canon; it **recognised** books that
already carried God's authority. The main tests were:

- **Apostolic** — written by an apostle or a close associate (like Mark and Luke).
- **Orthodox** — consistent with the faith already taught.
- **Widely received** — used and trusted by churches across the world.

The four Gospels and Paul's letters were widely accepted by the mid-to-late second
century. Later writings like the *Gospel of Thomas* or *Gospel of Judas* didn't meet
these tests. In AD 367, Athanasius, a bishop in Alexandria, listed the 27 books we have
today — recognising what the church had largely received already.
