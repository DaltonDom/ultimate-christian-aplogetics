Most religions rest on teachings. Christianity rests on an **event**. The apostle Paul
said so directly: if Jesus did not rise from the dead, Christian faith is worthless
(1 Corinthians 15:14, 17).

That is a remarkable thing to say. Paul put all his eggs in one historical basket. If
someone could produce Jesus' body, the whole faith would collapse. This means Christianity
is **falsifiable**: in principle, evidence could prove it false. That's a strength, not a
weakness. It invites people to investigate.

So when someone says, *"I'm not sure Christianity is true,"* you can say: *"Let's look at
the one thing it all hangs on."*

## The Minimal Facts Approach

One of the most helpful ways to discuss the resurrection is the **minimal facts
approach**, developed by the scholar **Gary Habermas** and presented with **Mike Licona**
in their book *The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus* (2004).

The idea is simple. Instead of asking a skeptic to accept the whole Bible first, you use
only facts that meet two tests:

1. They are supported by **strong historical evidence**.
2. They are accepted by **the large majority of scholars** who study the subject,
   including skeptical and non-Christian ones.

It's like building your case using only evidence the other side already accepts.

To remember the facts, think of **the five D's**:

1. **Died.** Jesus died by crucifixion. This is reported in all four Gospels, by Paul, and
   by non-Christian writers such as the Roman historian **Tacitus** and the Jewish
   historian **Josephus**. Almost no serious historian doubts it.
2. **Disciples.** His followers sincerely believed they saw Him alive after His death.
   They went from hiding behind locked doors to preaching boldly in the same city where He
   had been executed, and they were willing to suffer for it.
3. **Destroyer.** Paul, who was violently persecuting the church, became a Christian and
   said it was because the risen Jesus appeared to him (Galatians 1:13-16; 1 Corinthians
   15:8-9). We have his own letters.
4. **Doubter.** James, the brother of Jesus, was a skeptic during Jesus' ministry (John
   7:5; Mark 3:21). Yet he appears in the early list of resurrection witnesses (1
   Corinthians 15:7) and became a leader of the Jerusalem church. Josephus records his
   execution in the AD 60s.
5. **Deserted tomb.** The tomb was found empty. Habermas treats this as a "plus one" fact.
   A majority of scholars accept it, but it isn't quite as universally held as the other
   four. The evidence is still good:
   - The disciples preached in **Jerusalem**, where a body could easily have been produced.
   - The first witnesses were **women**. In that culture their testimony was often given
     less weight, so it's an unlikely detail to invent.
   - The earliest Jewish response was *"the disciples stole the body"* (Matthew
     28:11-15). That response **assumes the tomb was empty**.

## The Earliest Report: 1 Corinthians 15

A **creed** is a short, memorised summary of belief that was passed on by word of mouth.
In 1 Corinthians 15:3-7, Paul says he "delivered" to the Corinthians what he had
"received." Those were the standard words for handing on a tradition.

The creed lists the facts in a tight, memorable form. Christ **died** for our sins, was
**buried**, was **raised** on the third day, and **appeared** to Peter, to the Twelve, to more
than 500 people at once, to James, and to all the apostles.

Why does this matter?

- Paul wrote 1 Corinthians around **AD 55**, about 20–25 years after the crucifixion.
- He likely **received** the creed much earlier, probably when he met Peter and James in
  Jerusalem, about three years after his conversion (Galatians 1:18-19).
- Many scholars, including skeptical ones, date the creed to **within a few years of the
  crucifixion**.

Paul adds that most of the 500 were "still alive" when he wrote. In effect, he's saying, *go
and ask them.* That is the opposite of a legend, which usually grows after the eyewitnesses
are gone.

## Testing the Alternatives

> Picture a **lock with five pins**. A key only opens the lock if it lifts every pin at
> once. Each of the five D's is a pin. Many theories are keys that lift one or two pins.
> Only one key lifts all five.

Here's how the main alternative theories do:

**1. Hallucination.** *"The disciples were grieving and imagined it."*
Hallucinations are **private**, like dreams. You can't share one with a friend, let alone
with 500 people. The appearances happened to groups, in different places, over weeks. And
hallucinations don't explain the **empty tomb** (the body would still be there) or **Paul**,
who was an enemy, not a grieving friend.

**2. Swoon.** *"Jesus didn't really die. He revived in the tomb."*
Roman soldiers were professional executioners, and John reports a spear thrust into His
side (John 19:34). Even if He had survived, a half-dead man limping out of a tomb would
inspire **pity**, not worship. The 19th-century skeptic David Strauss made this very point.
The theory also fails to explain Paul's conversion.

**3. Stolen body.** *"The disciples took it and lied."*
Then the disciples **knew** it was a lie, and yet they suffered and several were executed
for preaching it. People die for things they believe are true, but a group rarely suffers
for years for something they know they made up. It also doesn't explain Paul or James.

**4. Legend.** *"The story grew over time."*
The creed is **too early** and names **living witnesses**. Legends need generations to grow.
The resurrection message was being proclaimed within weeks, in the same city.

Only one explanation lifts all five pins: **God raised Jesus from the dead.**

Be honest about where the real disagreement lies. Historians can establish the facts. What
often stops a person from accepting the resurrection isn't the evidence. It's the belief that
miracles can't happen. That's a question about whether God exists (see *Does God Exist?* and
*Science, Evolution, and Miracles*). If there is a God who made the universe, raising one
man from the dead is not a problem for Him.

In a real conversation you won't cover all of this at once. Start with one question, such as
*"What do you think happened to Jesus' body?"* Let the person offer a theory, then gently test
it against the five D's. You're not trying to win. You're helping them see that the
resurrection is a reasonable conclusion, so that the real issue, what they'll do with Jesus,
comes into view.

## Why It Matters for the Person in Front of You

The resurrection isn't just a puzzle to solve. It's God's announcement to the world. In
Athens, Paul said God has fixed a day when He will judge the world by the man He appointed,
and He has given proof of this to everyone **by raising Him from the dead** (Acts 17:31).

So if Jesus rose:

1. **He is who He claimed to be:** the Son of God and Lord.
2. **His death really paid for sin.** God accepted the payment (Romans 4:25).
3. **Judgment is real.** The same Jesus will judge the world.
4. **Death is not the end** for those who trust Him (1 Corinthians 15:20).

This is where the evidence should lead: out of the history classroom and into the heart.
The empty tomb is both a **warning** (a Judge is coming) and an **invitation** (the Saviour is
alive, and forgiveness is offered to all who repent and trust Him).

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the five D's:** Died, Disciples, Destroyer, Doubter, Deserted tomb.
2. **Memorise 1 Corinthians 15:3-4** with the flashcards.
3. **Practise the five-pin lock** out loud. Explain it in under 60 seconds.
4. **Pick one alternative theory** (hallucination, swoon, stolen body, or legend) and
   practise explaining why it fails, using the drills.
5. **Ask someone this week:** *"What do you think happened to Jesus' body?"* Then listen, and
   look for a way to move from the history to the gospel.
