Ask ten people whether they're a good person and nine will say yes. Ask them *why*, and
you'll usually hear some version of this: *"Well, I've never killed anyone. I'm not like
those people on the news."*

Notice what just happened. They measured themselves against the **worst** people they
could think of. By that measure almost everyone passes. It's like a student who fails
every exam but feels fine because someone in the class set the building on fire.

In the last lesson we saw that the Law works like a **mirror**: it shows us our sin so we
run to Christ. This lesson is about how to hold that mirror up. It means knowing the Ten
Commandments well, understanding how Jesus applied them, and learning which ones to use
in a real conversation.

## The Ten in Plain Language

The Ten Commandments are found in **Exodus 20:1-17**. God gave them to Israel at Mount
Sinai, but their moral heart runs through the whole Bible and is written on every human
conscience (more on that in the next lesson). Here they are in everyday words, each with
the question it asks:

1. **No other gods.** *Has God been first in your life, above everything and everyone?*
2. **No idols.** *Have you made up a god you're comfortable with, one who "would never"
   judge anyone?*
3. **Don't misuse God's name.** *Have you used His name carelessly, or as a swear word?*
4. **Keep the Sabbath.** *Have you set apart time for the God who gave you every day
   you've had?*
5. **Honour your parents.** *Have you always treated your mother and father with respect?*
6. **Don't murder.** *Have you ever hated someone?*
7. **Don't commit adultery.** *Have you been faithful in body and in mind?*
8. **Don't steal.** *Have you taken anything that wasn't yours, whatever its value?*
9. **Don't lie.** *Have you ever told a lie?*
10. **Don't covet.** *Have you craved what belongs to someone else?*

(A side note: Catholics and Lutherans number the commandments slightly differently,
joining the first two and splitting the last. The content is the same, so don't get
stuck on the numbering.)

The first four are about how we treat **God**. The last six are about how we treat
**people**. Jesus summed up both halves as loving God with everything and loving your
neighbour as yourself (Matthew 22:37-40). When you put it that way, nobody comes close.

## Jesus Turned the Volume Up

Many people in Jesus' day thought they were keeping the Law. If you hadn't killed anyone
or slept with someone else's spouse, you were fine. Then in the Sermon on the Mount
Jesus showed them what the commandments had meant all along.

- **Murder.** *"You have heard that it was said… 'You shall not murder'… But I say to you
  that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment."* Hateful
  anger and contempt ("You fool!") are **murder of the heart**. John later says the same
  thing: whoever hates his brother is a murderer (1 John 3:15).
- **Adultery.** Jesus said that anyone who looks at a woman **with lustful intent** has
  already committed adultery with her **in his heart**. What we imagine and want counts
  too.

Jesus wasn't inventing new rules. He was showing the **true depth** of the old ones.
People look at the outside, but God looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Acts begin as
desires, and God judges both.

This matters a great deal in evangelism. Almost no one you meet has committed murder or
physical adultery. But nearly everyone has been consumed by anger at someone, and almost
every adult has looked with lust. When you share how Jesus explained these commands,
the mirror suddenly shows much more.

> Imagine every thought you had this week projected onto a screen for your family to
> watch. Most of us would want to leave the room. God has seen all of it already.

## One Broken Link

People often think of sin like a score. A few bad deeds sit on one side, a lot of good
deeds on the other, and as long as the good side is heavier they expect to pass.

James takes that idea apart: *"Whoever keeps the whole law but fails in one point has
become guilty of all of it"* (James 2:10).

Why? Because the Law isn't a set of separate tests. It's the expression of **one holy
God's will**. Break any part of it and you've defied the One who gave all of it.

> Picture yourself hanging over a cliff on a chain of ten links. How many links need to
> break before you fall? Just one.

This is also why good deeds can't cancel out bad ones. If you're convicted of a crime,
the judge won't let you go because you've also done some kind things. Good deeds are
what we should have been doing all along. They don't pay for what we've done wrong.

## The Four You'll Use Most

You don't need to go through all ten in a conversation. In practice, a handful of
commandments do most of the work because nearly everyone recognises them straight away.
These four are a good place to start:

- **Lying (the ninth).** *"Have you ever told a lie?"* It's easy to ask and almost
  everyone says yes. Then gently ask, *"What do you call someone who lies?"* and let them
  answer.
- **Stealing (the eighth).** *"Have you ever taken something that didn't belong to you,
  even something small?"* Downloads, office supplies, and "borrowed" things never
  returned all count. Value doesn't matter. Theft is theft.
- **Blasphemy (the third).** *"Have you ever used God's name as a swear word?"* Explain
  kindly why it matters: we'd never use our own mother's name as a curse word, yet we use
  the name of the God who gave us life to express disgust. Many people have never
  thought about it and are genuinely struck by it.
- **Adultery of the heart (the seventh).** *"Jesus said that looking with lust is
  adultery of the heart. Have you ever done that?"* Handle this one with care. Keep it
  brief and never ask for details. It's usually wisest to use it one-on-one and with
  someone of the same sex, and to skip it altogether when children are around.

A few tips for using them well:

- **Ask, then pause.** Let the person answer. The silence is where the conscience speaks.
- **Include yourself.** *"Me too"* is honest, and it keeps you from sounding superior.
- **Don't rush past the answer.** After two or three, ask the key question: *"If God
  judged you by this standard, would you be innocent or guilty?"*
- **Don't argue about every sin.** If someone insists they've never lied, move on to the
  next commandment. You don't need them to admit to all ten.

## Why Specifics Beat "Nobody's Perfect"

It's tempting to skip all this and just say, *"We're all sinners."* Most people will
agree, and then shrug. *"Nobody's perfect"* sounds like an excuse, not a confession.

Specific commandments work differently. Paul said he wouldn't have known what coveting was
if the Law hadn't said "You shall not covet" (Romans 7:7). A general idea of sin didn't
trouble him. A specific commandment did.

It's the same with people today. *"I'm not perfect"* costs nothing to say. *"I'm a liar
and a thief, and I've blasphemed God's name"* is different, and it's that kind of
honesty that makes someone want a Saviour.

Always remember what the mirror is for. You aren't trying to win, and you aren't
building a case to condemn anyone. You're helping a friend see what's true so the
cross will make sense. Once they see it, take them to the good news straight away.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Learn the Ten in order** using the plain-language list above. Say them out loud
   until you can do it in under a minute.
2. **Memorise James 2:10** and practise the chain illustration.
3. **Read Matthew 5:21-30** slowly and ask God to use it as a mirror on your own heart
   before you use it on anyone else's.
4. **Rehearse the four questions** (lying, stealing, blasphemy, lust) with a Christian
   friend, including the follow-up: *"Innocent or guilty?"*
5. **Watch for comparisons** this week, like "I'm not as bad as…" They're a natural
   opening to talk about God's standard.
