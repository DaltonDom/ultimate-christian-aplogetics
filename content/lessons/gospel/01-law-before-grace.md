Imagine a doctor walks into the waiting room and starts handing out expensive
medicine to everyone. Most people would politely refuse. *"I feel fine,"* they'd say.
*"Why would I take that?"*

But if that same doctor sat you down, showed you your scan, and gently explained that
you had a serious disease, you would **beg** for the cure.

That's the heart of this lesson. The good news of Jesus is a cure. But people rarely
value a cure until they understand the disease. In the Bible, the tool God uses to show
us the disease is **His Law** — His moral standard, summed up in the Ten Commandments.

## The Problem with "Just Believe"

A lot of well-meaning evangelism goes something like this: *"Jesus will give you peace,
joy, and purpose. Just invite Him into your life."*

Everything in that sentence might be true — but notice what's missing. There's nothing
about **sin**, nothing about **why** anyone needs Jesus, and nothing about **what He
rescues us from**. People hear it as a lifestyle upgrade, like a new gym membership.

When life gets hard (and it will), a person who "tried Jesus" for happiness often walks
away, because they were promised a better life, not forgiveness. But a person who
understood they were guilty before a holy God, and that Jesus paid their fine, holds on,
because they know what He saved them from.

> The question isn't "Would you like a better life?" It's "What will you do on the day
> you stand before a holy God?"

## What the Law Is For

The **Law** is God's moral standard: don't lie, don't steal, don't murder, don't commit
adultery, love God with everything, and so on. The Bible says the Law is **good** (Romans
7:12), but it also says the Law **cannot save anyone** (Romans 3:20). So what is it for?

Paul gives a clear answer: *"through the law comes knowledge of sin."* The Law works like
a **mirror**:

- A mirror **shows** you that your face is dirty.
- A mirror **can't clean** your face.
- A mirror **sends you** to the water.

In the same way, the Law shows us that we've broken God's standard. It can't forgive us.
But it sends us running to the only One who can — Jesus.

That's what Galatians 3:24 means when it calls the Law our **"guardian"** (some
translations say "tutor" or "schoolmaster"). In Paul's day a guardian was a household
servant who walked a child to their teacher. The Law walks us to Christ.

## How Jesus Did It

It helps to notice how Jesus Himself spoke to different people:

- **The proud and self-assured** got the Law. When the rich young ruler asked how to
  inherit eternal life, Jesus pointed him to the commandments (Mark 10:17-22). The man
  claimed he'd kept them all, so Jesus pressed on the one thing he loved more than God.
- **The broken and humble** got grace. With the woman at the well (John 4), the thief on
  the cross (Luke 23), and Zacchaeus (Luke 19), Jesus quickly offered mercy — they
  already knew they were sinners.

A simple way to remember this: **Law to the proud, grace to the humble.**

Most people you meet in everyday life will say something like, *"I'm a pretty good
person."* That tells you where to start. They don't need to hear about grace yet,
because they don't think they need it. They need a mirror.

## The Law Makes Grace Amazing

Here's the best part: the Law isn't bad news for its own sake. It's what makes the good
news **good**.

Think of it like a courtroom. You're guilty of serious crimes, and the fine is more than
you could ever pay. The judge is good, so he can't just let you walk. Then someone you've
never met steps forward and pays the entire fine. You're free to go.

If you didn't know you were guilty, that payment would mean nothing to you. But once you
understand the verdict, it becomes the most wonderful thing that has ever happened to
you. That's what the Law does. **It makes grace amazing.**

This is why Christians throughout history have preached both. Martin Luther, Charles
Spurgeon, George Whitefield, and many others were known for using the Law to prepare
people's hearts for the gospel. In recent decades, Ray Comfort and the Living Waters team
have helped a new generation recover this approach through what's often called "The Way
of the Master."

## Doing It with Love

Using the Law does not mean being harsh. The goal is not to win, embarrass, or shame
anyone. It's to help someone see the truth so they can be rescued. A few principles:

- **Ask, don't accuse.** "Have you ever told a lie?" lets their conscience answer.
  "You're a liar" puts them on the defensive.
- **Include yourself.** "I've done that too" keeps you on the same level — because you are.
- **Keep your tone light and kind.** You're a fellow beggar showing another beggar where
  to find bread.
- **Don't stop at the Law.** Once someone sees their guilt, move to the cross. The Law is
  the needle; the gospel is the thread. You never leave someone with just the needle.
