*"All roads lead to the same place."* *"Whatever works for you."* *"I'm spiritual, just not
religious."* You've probably heard these more than any argument from a professional
atheist. They sound kind, open-minded, and humble. And if you disagree, you can come
across as narrow and judgmental.

But underneath these friendly phrases are real ideas about truth and God — and they don't
hold together as well as they seem. This lesson will help you understand the people who
say them, question the ideas gently, and point to the One who claimed to be "the truth."

## What They Actually Believe

These phrases usually come from one of three related ideas:

- **Truth relativism:** *"That's true for you, but not for me."* Truth depends on the
  person or culture, rather than on how things really are.
- **Moral relativism:** *"Who are you to say what's right?"* Right and wrong are personal
  or cultural preferences, not universal facts.
- **Religious pluralism:** *"All paths lead to God."* Every sincere religion is an equally
  valid way to the same reality.

The **"spiritual but not religious"** (often shortened to SBNR) usually believe there's
*something more* — God, the universe, energy, a higher self — and they may pray, meditate,
practise yoga, read horoscopes, or feel close to God in nature. What they reject is
**religion**: institutions, rules, creeds, and churches. Many were hurt by church, or
watched religious people behave badly. They'd describe themselves as **open, tolerant,
and on a personal journey**.

Understand this: for most people, these beliefs aren't a carefully built philosophy.
They're a way of **keeping the peace** in a diverse world, and of keeping spirituality
personal and free.

## Common Ground

There's a lot you can honestly affirm:

- **There is more to life than the material world.** They're right about that.
- **Humility matters.** None of us knows everything.
- **Religious hypocrisy is real** — and Jesus opposed it fiercely.
- **People should be treated with respect**, whatever they believe.
- **The longing for meaning is real.** Ecclesiastes 3:11 says God "put eternity into
  man's heart." That spiritual hunger is a signpost.

Tell them what you agree with first. It shows you've listened.

## Showing the Cracks — Gently

Many relativist statements are **self-refuting**: they undermine themselves. You don't
point this out to score points but to help your friend see that everyone believes in some
truth.

- *"There is no absolute truth."* — Is that absolutely true?
- *"It's wrong to say someone else is wrong."* — Then isn't that statement wrong too?
- *"You shouldn't force your views on others."* — Isn't that a view you're asking me to
  accept?

Then there's the idea that all religions are basically the same. In fact, **every major
religion makes exclusive claims**:

- Christianity says Jesus is God the Son, who died for sin and rose again.
- Islam teaches that God has no son, and mainstream Muslim teaching holds that Jesus was
  not crucified.
- Classical Buddhism doesn't teach a personal Creator God, and seeks release from
  suffering rather than forgiveness of sin.
- Many forms of Hinduism teach reincarnation and that the self is ultimately one with
  divine reality.

They can't all be true, because they contradict each other. They could all be false — or
one could be true. Even pluralism is exclusive: it says every religion that claims to be
uniquely true is wrong.

> Real tolerance isn't pretending we all agree. It's disagreeing honestly and treating
> each other with respect anyway. You can only tolerate someone you disagree with.

## Questions for Relativists and Seekers

Good questions keep things friendly and help your friend think:

1. **"What does being spiritual look like for you?"** Shows interest and uncovers what
   they actually believe.
2. **"How did you come to see things that way?"** Often reveals a story — including church
   hurt you can respond to with compassion.
3. **"Is that true for everyone, or just for you?"** A gentle way to expose a
   self-refuting claim.
4. **"If all paths lead to God, which God? Are the God of Islam and the gods of Hinduism
   the same?"** Helps them see how differently religions describe God.
5. **"Is anything wrong for everyone, everywhere — like torturing a child for fun?"**
   Almost everyone says yes, which shows moral relativism isn't how they really live.
6. **"What do you think God is like — and how would you know?"** Moves from feelings to
   the question of revelation.
7. **"If Jesus really rose from the dead, would that change things?"** Brings the
   conversation to a historical claim, not a preference.
8. **"Have you lived up to your own standard of being a good person?"** Opens the door to
   the conscience and the Law.

## Sharing the Gospel with Relativists and Seekers

Here's the key insight: **the relativist doesn't mainly need a philosophy lesson.** They
need to see that they have a problem no path can fix.

- **Use their own moral standard.** Even relativists care about honesty and kindness. Ask
  if they've always lived up to it. Then take them to God's standard, the Ten
  Commandments.
- **Show the difference between religion and the gospel.** Every other path is about what
  *we* do to reach God. The gospel is about what *God* did to reach us. That's why Jesus
  isn't one path among many — He is the only one who dealt with sin.
- **Make Jesus the issue.** Most SBNR people admire Jesus. Point out that He made the
  boldest claims of all (John 14:6), and that the resurrection is how He backed them up.
  You can't treat Him as just a wise teacher if He claimed to be the only way.
- **Listen for the hurt.** Many "spiritual but not religious" people walked away from
  church after being judged, ignored, or even abused. Don't defend what shouldn't be
  defended. Say, *"I'm really sorry that happened. That's not what Jesus is like."* Then
  gently separate Jesus from the people who misrepresented Him. Your kindness may be the
  first evidence they've seen in years that His followers can be trusted.
- **Invite them to seek honestly.** Encourage them to read a Gospel, asking God to show
  them if Jesus is who He said He was. The Gospel of John is a great place to start,
  because it was written "so that you may believe" (John 20:31).

## Do's and Don'ts

**Do:**

- Affirm their spiritual hunger — it's real.
- Ask questions before you make points.
- Point out self-contradictions gently, with a smile.
- Admit that Christians have sometimes behaved badly.
- Keep bringing it back to Jesus Himself.

**Don't:**

- Mock horoscopes, crystals, or yoga. Understand before you critique.
- Get stuck in abstract philosophy for the whole conversation.
- Act as if exclusive truth means you're better than them. You're a sinner saved by
  grace.
- Pretend all religions are "basically the same" to avoid offence. That's not kind.
- Forget the goal: not winning the argument, but pointing them to the cross.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise John 14:6** this week with the flashcards.
2. **Practise one self-refutation question** ("Is that true for everyone?") until you can
   ask it warmly, not smugly.
3. **Learn one example** of how two religions contradict each other, so you can explain
   why they can't all be true.
4. **Listen for "spiritual but not religious"** this week, and respond with, "What does
   that look like for you?"
5. **Pray** for a spiritually curious friend, that their hunger would lead them to Jesus.
