You're in a yoga class, a meditation app is playing softly on your coworker's phone, and your
neighbour talks about "good karma" and "past lives" as naturally as the weather. Millions of
people in the West now hold ideas that come from Eastern religions — often without belonging
to any temple at all. And millions more are devout Hindus and Buddhists.

How do you share Jesus with someone who thinks in terms of karma, rebirth, and inner
enlightenment? You start by understanding them, and you finish with the one word their system
has no room for: **grace**.

## What They Actually Believe

These are three distinct families of belief, each with enormous variety. Always ask your
friend what *they* believe. But here are the basics most would recognise.

**Hinduism** is the world's oldest major religion and is more a family of traditions than one
system.

- **Brahman** is ultimate reality. Some Hindus (especially in the Advaita tradition) see
  Brahman as **impersonal** and believe the true self (**atman**) is ultimately one with it.
  Others practise **bhakti**, loving devotion to a personal deity like Krishna, Vishnu, or
  Shiva. Many see the many gods as expressions of one divine reality.
- **Karma** is the moral law of cause and effect: your actions shape your future, including
  your next life.
- **Samsara** is the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. The goal is **moksha** — liberation
  from the cycle — through knowledge, devotion, or right action.

**Buddhism** began with Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha ("the awakened one"), around 500 BC.

- The **Four Noble Truths**: life involves suffering and dissatisfaction (**dukkha**); it is
  caused by craving; it can end; and the way to end it is the **Eightfold Path** of right
  view, right conduct, and right meditation.
- Most Buddhists do not believe in a creator God. Many teach **no permanent self** (anatta)
  — yet rebirth still continues, driven by karma.
- The goal is **nirvana**: the "blowing out" of craving and the end of the cycle of rebirth.
  Traditions vary widely, from Theravada in Southeast Asia to Mahayana and Tibetan Buddhism.

**New Age spirituality** is a Western mix: the divine is within you, everything is connected
energy, "the universe" responds to your intentions, and reincarnation is often seen as a
positive journey of growth. Truth is usually treated as personal: "whatever works for you."

## Common Ground

You can agree with much, and you should say so:

- The **physical world isn't all there is.**
- **Our choices matter** morally. (The Bible agrees you reap what you sow — Galatians 6:7.)
- **Suffering is real** and deep, and life in this world is not as it should be.
- **Selfish craving** is a real problem.
- The spiritual life takes **discipline**, humility, and compassion.

Paul modelled this in Athens (Acts 17). He was troubled by the idols, but he started with
what his hearers already sensed — that they were seeking something — and then introduced them
to the God they did not know.

## The Differences That Matter for Salvation

**1. Is God a "Someone" or a "Something"?** For many Eastern and New Age thinkers, ultimate
reality is impersonal — a force, an energy, a oneness. The Bible reveals a **personal** God
who speaks, loves, forgives, and can be known. An energy field can't love you or forgive you.

**2. One life or many?** Reincarnation offers many lives to get it right. The Bible says we
live **once** and then face judgment (Hebrews 9:27). The Christian hope is not reincarnation
but **resurrection**: the same person, raised in a renewed body, forever.

**3. Karma or grace?** Karma is a perfect accounting system. Every wrong must be paid for —
by you. Grace is when God pays it Himself.

> Think of karma as a debt that you must pay off in endless instalments, with no statement
> telling you the balance. Grace is a letter that says: **"Paid in full."** Someone else has
> covered it.

**4. What's our problem?** In much Eastern thought, the deepest problem is **ignorance** or
**illusion**, and the answer is enlightenment. The Bible says our deepest problem is **sin** —
real moral rebellion against a holy God — and the answer is forgiveness.

## The Problem of Suffering

Suffering is often the doorway into these conversations. Buddhism was born from it. Karma
explains it: suffering is the fruit of past deeds. That can sound fair, but ask where it leads.
It means a child born into poverty or with a disability somehow deserved it. It can also make
people passive about the suffering of others.

Jesus flatly rejected that reasoning. When His disciples asked whose sin caused a man's
blindness, Jesus said it was neither his nor his parents' — and then He healed him (John
9:1-3). Christianity says suffering entered through humanity's rebellion, but that not every
suffering is personal payback. Most of all, it says God did not stay distant from our pain.
In Jesus, He **entered** it, suffered with us and for us, and rose to guarantee that suffering
will one day end (Revelation 21:4).

## Questions to Ask

Questions help people discover the gaps in their own views without feeling attacked. Choose a
few that fit the person:

1. **"Who or what keeps the records of karma?"** A moral law suggests a moral lawgiver.
2. **"How would you know when you've paid off all your bad karma?"**
3. **"If karma is perfectly fair, is there any room for mercy?"**
4. **"If I'm suffering for a past life I can't remember, how can I learn from it?"**
5. **"Is ultimate reality a 'someone' or a 'something'? Can a something love you?"**
6. **"If the divine is within you, why do we all struggle with selfishness and guilt?"**
7. **"If everyone creates their own truth, could anyone's truth ever be wrong?"** (Especially
   useful with New Age friends.)
8. **"What would it mean to you if freedom weren't earned over many lifetimes, but given as
   a gift now?"**

## Sharing Grace with People Trained to Earn

Your friend has probably spent years trying to accumulate good karma, meditate enough, or
detach from desire. Grace will sound strange — even too good to be true. Here's how to help:

- **Start with the Law.** Karma-minded people already believe wrongs have consequences. Gently
  walk through a few of the Ten Commandments. The goal is to show that the debt is too great
  for any number of lifetimes.
- **Present a personal God.** Explain that the one who made us is not a force but a Father who
  knows us by name.
- **Show the cross as payment.** Jesus didn't just teach a path; He paid the debt. Then He
  rose bodily, proving His claims.
- **Tell the story of the prodigal son** (Luke 15:11-32). A son who deserved nothing is met by
  a father who runs to him. It is the opposite of karma.
- **Be clear about uniqueness.** Many Hindus will happily add Jesus to their gods. Gently
  explain that He claimed to be the only way (John 14:6) and that following Him means turning
  from all others.

## Respectful Do's and Don'ts

**Do:**

- **Ask about their tradition** and family. Many Hindus and Buddhists have rich cultural
  identities tied to their faith.
- **Accept hospitality** and respect food customs (many are vegetarian).
- **Share your own story** of grace — testimony is powerful here.
- **Be patient.** Following Jesus may cost your friend deeply with family.

**Don't:**

- **Mock images, temples, or meditation.**
- **Assume** every Hindu, Buddhist, or New Ager believes the same things.
- **Let "all paths are the same" go unexamined** — gently show the differences.
- **Present Jesus as a technique** for inner peace. Present Him as Lord and Saviour.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise Titus 3:5** — "not because of works done by us."
2. **Practise the debt illustration** until you can explain karma versus grace in 30 seconds.
3. **Learn the key terms** — karma, samsara, moksha, nirvana, dukkha — so you can listen well.
4. **Try one question** from the list the next time someone mentions karma or past lives.
5. **Pray** for someone you know who is spiritually searching, that they would find rest in
   Christ.
