  - q: "Protestants made up 'faith alone.' James 2:24 says we're not justified by faith alone."
    a: >-
      That's a fair verse to raise, and it deserves a real answer. James is correcting people
      who claimed to have faith but showed no change at all — a "faith" that is only words
      (James 2:14-17). His point is that genuine faith always shows itself in works. Paul's
      point is that works are never the ground of our standing with God (Romans 4:4-5).
      Evangelicals say it this way: we are saved by faith alone, but saving faith is never
      alone. Works are the fruit of salvation, not the root.

    a: >-
      I'm genuinely grateful for the early church that recognised and preserved the books of
      Scripture. But recognising something isn't the same as creating its authority. The
      books were God-breathed from the moment they were written — the church received them,
      the way a son recognises his father's handwriting. That's why Scripture can stand over
      every later tradition, including the traditions of any church, mine included.
  - q: "Catholics don't worship Mary. We honour her, like you'd honour your mother."
    a: >-
      Thank you for clarifying — I don't want to misrepresent you. The Catholic Church
      distinguishes worship given to God from the honour given to Mary and the saints.
      My concern is less about the word and more about the practice: praying to her,
      trusting her intercession, and calling her sinless go beyond anything Scripture
      teaches. Mary herself called God her "Savior" (Luke 1:47) and pointed people to
      Jesus: "Do whatever he tells you" (John 2:5).
  - q: "Aren't you just being divisive? Can't we focus on Jesus?"
    a: >-
      I'd love to focus on Jesus — that's exactly why I care about this. We agree on who He
      is. The question is what He accomplished: did His death fully pay for my sins, or
      does it make salvation possible if I then do my part? That's not a small difference;
      it changes whether I can have peace with God today.

You're chatting with a friend who crosses herself before meals, has a rosary in her car, and
knows the Apostles' Creed by heart. She believes Jesus is God, that He died for sins, and that
He rose from the dead. So is there anything to talk about?

Yes — and it may be the most important conversation you ever have with her. But how you
approach it matters. You are not talking to someone who has never heard of Jesus. You are
talking to someone who professes faith in Him, and who shares far more with you than many
people realise.

## Start with What You Share

Evangelicals and Roman Catholics share the great **creeds** of the early church — the
Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed. Both believe:

- There is **one God** in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (the **Trinity**).
- Jesus is **fully God and fully man**, born of the virgin Mary.
- He **died on the cross** for sinners, **rose bodily** from the dead, and **will return** to
  judge the living and the dead.
- The Bible is the inspired **Word of God**.
- Human life is sacred, marriage is holy, and sin is real.

Catholic Christians have stood alongside evangelicals on many issues, and many have
courageously suffered for their faith. So approach your Catholic friend with warmth, not
suspicion. Say "we" when you honestly can.

That said, some of the differences are not minor. They touch the very heart of the gospel:
**how a sinner is made right with God.**

## Difference 1: How We Are Justified

To be **justified** means to be declared righteous — "not guilty" — before God.

**Catholic teaching** (defined at the Council of Trent in the 1500s and summarised in the
Catechism) says justification is by God's grace, and that no one can earn it on their own. But
it describes justification as a *process*: God's grace is poured into a person, beginning at
**baptism**, and it grows through the **sacraments** (such as the Mass and confession) and
through grace-enabled good works. A serious ("mortal") sin can lose that grace, which is then
restored through the sacrament of confession. Catholic teaching also says a person cannot
normally be certain of final salvation.

**Evangelical teaching** says justification is a *verdict*: the moment a sinner trusts Christ,
God declares them righteous, not because of anything in them, but because Christ's
righteousness is **credited** to them. This is **faith alone** (sola fide). Good works follow
as the fruit of salvation, not the root.

Notice that both sides use the word **grace**. The difference is whether grace *enables us to
contribute* to our justification, or whether Christ has *already done it all*.

> Imagine a friend pays off your mortgage in full. Would you keep sending monthly payments
> "just to be safe"? If you did, you'd be saying the payment wasn't really enough. The cross
> is a payment in full.

## Difference 2: Where Final Authority Lies

**Catholic teaching** says God's revelation comes through **Scripture** and **Sacred
Tradition**, both authoritatively interpreted by the **Magisterium** (the teaching office of
the Pope and bishops).

**Evangelicals** hold to **Scripture alone** (sola Scriptura). This doesn't mean ignoring
tradition — we treasure the creeds and the wisdom of the church. It means Scripture is the
**only infallible** and **final** authority, and every tradition must be tested by it. Jesus
Himself tested the religious traditions of His day by Scripture (Mark 7:8-13).

## Difference 3: Mary

Everyone should honour Mary. She was chosen, faithful, and blessed among women (Luke 1:42). But Catholic
teaching goes further: that Mary was conceived **without original sin** (the Immaculate
Conception), was **taken bodily into heaven** (the Assumption), and **intercedes** for those
who pray to her. Catholics carefully distinguish this honour from the worship given to God, and
you should acknowledge that distinction. Evangelicals simply see no biblical basis for these
teachings, and believe prayer belongs to God alone through the one mediator, Jesus (1 Timothy
2:5).

## Difference 4: Purgatory

Catholic teaching says those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified undergo a
final cleansing called **purgatory** before entering heaven. Evangelicals believe Christ's
sacrifice has fully dealt with our sin, and that believers go straight into Christ's presence
at death (Luke 23:43; Philippians 1:23). If there is still purification to endure, it becomes
hard to see how the cross was a payment in full.

## Questions That Get to the Heart

As always, ask more than you tell. Many Catholics — like many Protestants — have never
thought carefully about what their church teaches. Good questions help your friend think
about what they are really trusting in:

1. **"If you died tonight and God asked, 'Why should I let you into heaven?', what would you
   say?"** Listen for whether the answer is about Christ or about their own effort.
2. **"How confident are you that you'd be with God? Why?"**
3. **"What do you think grace means?"**
4. **"When Jesus said 'It is finished,' what do you think was finished?"**
5. **"Do you see your good works as the root of your salvation or the fruit of it?"**
6. **"If a church teaching and the Bible seemed to disagree, which would you trust?"**
7. **"Have you ever read Romans or Galatians straight through?"**

Your aim is not to get them to leave a church. It is to help them trust **Christ alone**.

## Sharing the Gospel with a Catholic Friend

Don't assume a Catholic friend already understands the gospel just because they know the
creeds — and don't assume they don't. Many Catholics have heard about sin and grace all their
lives but still feel they have to "keep up." Others are already resting in Jesus alone.

The Law is still useful here. Many Catholics believe they are "basically good" with a few
sins to confess. Gently walking through the Ten Commandments can help them see that the
problem isn't a few bad habits but a guilty heart — and that no amount of effort, ritual, or
purification could ever fix it. Then show them the wonder of a **finished** cross and a
**gift** received by faith.

## Respectful Do's and Don'ts

**Do:**

- **Speak charitably.** Represent Catholic teaching the way a thoughtful Catholic would.
- **Use Scripture.** Most Catholics respect the Bible deeply. Invite them to read it with you.
- **Celebrate shared truth** — sincerely.
- **Focus on the gospel,** not on every issue at once.

**Don't:**

- **Use insults** or polemical labels. You want to persuade a friend, not win a war.
- **Bring up historical scandals** to score points — every tradition has sinners.
- **Assume** every Catholic believes every official teaching, or that none of them know
  Christ.
- **Make it about switching churches.** Make it about trusting Jesus.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise Ephesians 2:8-9** and be ready to explain "not a result of works."
2. **Practise the diagnostic question** — "Why should God let you into heaven?" — until it
   feels natural to ask.
3. **Learn the four key differences** from the flashcards so you can explain them fairly.
4. **Invite a Catholic friend** to read Romans or Galatians with you over a few weeks.
5. **Pray** for Catholic friends and family by name — that they would rest fully in Christ's
   finished work.
