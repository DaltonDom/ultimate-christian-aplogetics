Imagine two Christians who work in the same office. They believe the same things and
could give the same answers to hard questions. But when coworkers want to talk about
God, they only ever go to one of them.

Why? The first one argues, interrupts, and always needs to win. The second one listens,
asks good questions, admits when she doesn't know something, and is genuinely kind —
even to people who mock her faith.

Same truth. Different **posture**. And posture decides whether people ever hear the
truth at all.

## Gracious Speech, Seasoned with Salt

Paul gives a short job description for every Christian in Colossians 4:5-6. Look at each
phrase:

- **"Walk in wisdom toward outsiders."** The way we live around unbelievers matters.
  They are watching.
- **"Making the best use of the time."** Opportunities come and go. Don't waste them —
  but don't force them either.
- **"Let your speech always be gracious."** Gracious means kind, warm, and generous —
  treating people better than they may deserve, the way God treated us.
- **"Seasoned with salt."** Salt adds flavour and keeps food from spoiling. Our words
  shouldn't be bland or preachy; they should be interesting and honest, with a bit of
  bite when needed.
- **"So that you may know how you ought to answer each person."** Notice: *each*
  person. There's no single script. A grieving widow and a cocky student need different
  words.

Peter says the same thing in 1 Peter 3:15-16: be ready with a reason, *"yet do it with
gentleness and respect, having a good conscience."* **Gentleness** is how you treat the
person. **Respect** (literally "fear") includes reverence toward God — you are speaking
on His behalf, so speak in a way that honours Him.

Respect toward people flows from something deeper. Every person you talk to — the
friendly neighbour, the angry online commenter, the atheist professor — is made in the
**image of God** (Genesis 1:27). That gives them real dignity, even when they're wrong,
and even when they're rude. You can disagree completely with what someone believes and
still treat them as someone God made and Jesus came to save. In fact, if you really
believe that, you'll be both more honest and more kind: honest because their soul is at
stake, and kind because they are precious to God.

## The Lord's Servant Is Not Quarrelsome

In 2 Timothy 2:24-26, Paul describes the kind of person God uses to reach opponents of
the faith:

1. **Not quarrelsome.** Not looking for a fight.
2. **Kind to everyone.** Not just to the people who are nice back.
3. **Able to teach.** Knows the truth well enough to explain it simply.
4. **Patiently enduring evil.** Doesn't retaliate when mistreated.
5. **Correcting with gentleness.** Still corrects — but gently.

Then Paul explains *why*: *"God may perhaps grant them repentance."* Repentance is God's
gift. We can't force it by winning an argument or raising our voice. Our job is to be
the kind of messenger God delights to use.

Paul also reveals who people who oppose the truth really are. They are caught in *"the
snare of the devil."* That changes everything. The person mocking you is not your enemy;
they are a **captive**. You don't shout at a hostage. You work for their rescue.

> Gentleness is not weakness. It's strength under control — like a powerful horse that
> responds to the lightest touch of the reins.

Gentleness also doesn't mean hiding the hard parts. You will still talk about sin,
judgment, and the need to repent. In fact, the kindest thing you can do is tell someone
the truth about their danger. Gentleness is about **how** you tell it.

## Listening Is Half the Witness

*"Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger"* (James 1:19). And
Proverbs 18:13 adds that answering before you've heard is "folly and shame."

Many of us are so eager to give our answer that we don't hear the question. Good
listening looks like this:

- **Ask questions.** *"What do you mean by that?"* *"How did you come to that view?"*
- **Repeat back what you heard.** *"So you're saying you can't trust a book that was
  copied so many times — is that right?"* This shows respect and prevents you from
  answering something they never said.
- **Look for the story behind the question.** "Why does God allow suffering?" might be
  a philosophical puzzle — or it might mean *"My mum died last year."* The first needs
  an argument; the second needs compassion first.

People can tell when you're really listening, and it's rare. Listening says, *"You
matter to me — not just as a target, but as a person."*

## The Humility of "I Don't Know"

You will get asked questions you can't answer. That's okay. No one knows everything,
and pretending you do is one of the fastest ways to lose someone's trust.

Learn to say, with a smile:

> "That's a really good question. I don't know — but I'll find out and get back to you."

This simple sentence does four things:

1. It's **honest**, and honesty builds trust.
2. It shows you take their question **seriously**.
3. It gives you a natural reason for a **second conversation**.
4. It pushes you to **grow** — every question you can't answer is your next study topic.

The key is to **follow through**. Look it up, ask your pastor, check a trusted
resource, and then go back. Few things impress a sceptic more than a Christian who
actually returns with a thoughtful answer.

Humility also means admitting where Christians have been wrong. If someone was hurt by a
harsh or hypocritical believer, don't rush to defend the church. Say sorry for what was
genuinely wrong. Then point them to Jesus, who is nothing like the hypocrites.

## Your Life Is Part of the Argument

Jesus said, *"By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love
for one another"* (John 13:35). And He told us to let our light shine so that people see
our good works and give glory to the Father (Matthew 5:16).

Your character is an **apologetic** — a piece of evidence. When Christians are honest,
patient, generous, and kind, it makes the gospel believable. When we're harsh, proud,
or hypocritical, we hand people a reason to dismiss it. In the early centuries, pagan
observers sometimes remarked on how Christians cared for the sick and the poor —
including those who weren't Christians.

But be careful: your life **supports** the message; it doesn't **replace** it. You may
have heard "Preach the gospel; use words if necessary." It's often credited to Francis
of Assisi, though there's no good evidence he said it. Either way, words *are*
necessary: *"How are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard?"* (Romans
10:14). A kind life opens the door; the gospel must still be spoken.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise Colossians 4:5-6** this week and pray it before you go to work or school.
2. **In your next conversation, ask three questions** before you give any answers.
3. **Practise saying "I don't know, but I'll find out"** out loud until it feels natural
   — then keep a list of questions you need to follow up on.
4. **Think of someone who has been hurt by Christians.** Pray for them, and ask God for a
   chance to listen to their story.
5. **Ask a Christian friend** to tell you honestly how you come across when you talk
   about your faith.
