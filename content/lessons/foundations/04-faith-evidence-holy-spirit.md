objections:
  - q: "Isn't faith just believing something without evidence?"
    a: >-
      That's a common definition, but it isn't the Bible's. In Scripture, faith means
      trust — and trust is normally based on reasons. The apostles pointed to eyewitnesses,
      fulfilled prophecy, and the empty tomb. John says he wrote his Gospel so that
      people "may believe" (John 20:31). Evidence is meant to lead to faith, not replace it.
  - q: "If the evidence were really good, wouldn't everyone believe?"
    a: >-
      Not necessarily. People reject good evidence all the time when the conclusion would
      cost them something. Jesus said some would not be convinced even "if someone should
      rise from the dead" (Luke 16:31). Belief involves the will and the heart, not just
      the intellect — which is why the Holy Spirit's work is essential.
  - q: "If the Holy Spirit does the convincing, why do I need to give evidence?"
    a: >-
      Because the Spirit normally works through means — through Scripture, through
      preaching, and through reasons given by ordinary Christians. When Paul spoke to
      Lydia, "the Lord opened her heart to pay attention" to what Paul said (Acts 16:14).
      The Spirit didn't bypass Paul's words; He used them.
  - q: "Didn't Jesus say, \"Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed\"?"
    a: >-
      Yes (John 20:29) — but Thomas had plenty of reliable testimony from friends who had
      seen Jesus alive. The blessing is for people who trust trustworthy testimony without
      needing to see for themselves. And the very next verses say these signs were written
      down precisely so that we could believe (John 20:30-31).

differ:
  topic: Which apologetic method is best?
  views:
    - name: Classical apologetics
      summary: >-
        A two-step approach. First show that God exists using arguments from the
        universe's beginning, design, and morality; then show from history that Jesus
        rose from the dead and is God's Son. Associated with thinkers like R.C. Sproul,
        Norman Geisler, and William Lane Craig. Strength: builds a clear foundation.
        Critics worry it can take a long time to reach Jesus.
    - name: Evidential apologetics
      summary: >-
        Goes straight to historical evidence — especially the resurrection — arguing that
        if Jesus rose, that fact itself shows God exists and Jesus is who He claimed to
        be. Associated with Gary Habermas (the "minimal facts" approach) and John Warwick
        Montgomery. Strength: gets to Jesus fast. Critics worry that someone who rules
        out miracles in advance will explain away any evidence.
    - name: Presuppositional apologetics
      summary: >-
        Argues there is no truly neutral ground: everyone reasons from basic assumptions,
        and the unbeliever already knows God but suppresses that knowledge (Romans 1:18-21).
        Rather than letting the sceptic sit as judge over God, it challenges the sceptic's
        own foundations, arguing that logic, science, and morality only make sense if the
        God of the Bible exists. Associated with Cornelius Van Til and Greg Bahnsen.
        Strength: takes sin's effect on thinking seriously and puts Scripture first.
        Critics worry it can sound circular or dismissive of evidence; supporters reply
        that they do use evidence, but within a biblical framework.
    - name: Cumulative case apologetics
      summary: >-
        Works like a lawyer or detective: no single argument settles everything, but many
        strands — the universe's origin, its fine-tuning, morality, the life and
        resurrection of Jesus, changed lives — together are best explained by Christianity.
        Associated with thinkers such as Basil Mitchell, and in a popular way with C.S.
        Lewis. Strength: flexible and persuasive over time. Critics worry it can feel less
        decisive than a single knock-down argument.
  takeaway: >-
    All four camps agree on what matters most: Scripture is our final authority, sin
    affects the whole person, the Holy Spirit must open blind eyes, and the goal is the
    gospel. In practice, most seasoned witnesses borrow from each. Remember that people
    already know God is there (a presuppositional insight, and the heart of the conscience
    approach), give evidence to honest questions, build a cumulative case with thoughtful
    friends over time, and always move toward the cross. Choose the tool that fits the
    person in front of you.

Mark Twain once joked that faith is "believing what you know ain't so." Many people
today would agree. To them, *faith* means switching off your brain and believing
something with no good reason — or even against the evidence.

Sadly, some Christians talk as if that's true. *"You just have to have faith,"* they say,
when someone asks a hard question. But that's not what the Bible means by faith at all.

This lesson answers two important questions. **What is faith?** And **if the evidence is
good, why doesn't everyone believe?** The answers will shape how you talk about Jesus for
the rest of your life.

## What Biblical Faith Actually Is

The main New Testament word for faith (*pistis*) means **trust, confidence, or
reliance**. It's the same idea as trusting a friend, a doctor, or a bridge. And in normal
life, we trust things because we have good reason to.

Christians have long described saving faith as having three parts:

1. **Knowledge** — knowing what's true (Jesus died for sinners and rose again).
2. **Agreement** — being convinced it really is true.
3. **Trust** — personally relying on Jesus to save you.

Think of getting on a plane. You know what a plane is and what pilots do (knowledge).
You're convinced this airline and pilot are qualified (agreement). But you only fly when
you actually board and sit down (trust). Evidence gets you to the gate. Faith is getting
on the plane.

Hebrews 11:1 calls faith *"the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things
not seen."* **Assurance** and **conviction** are strong, confident words. The heroes
in Hebrews 11 weren't guessing. They trusted God because they knew who He was and what
He had promised.

> Faith doesn't mean "no evidence." It means trusting what the evidence points to — even
> when you can't see the whole picture yet.

## The Bible Invites Investigation

Christianity is unusual among religions in how much it rests on **public, historical
events**. The New Testament writers don't say, *"Just believe."* They keep pointing to
things that could be checked:

- **Luke** researched carefully, drawing on eyewitnesses, so his reader could have
  "certainty" (Luke 1:1-4).
- **Jesus** presented Himself alive after His death "by many proofs" over forty days
  (Acts 1:3).
- **Paul** listed witnesses of the risen Jesus, noting that most of them were still
  alive — in effect saying, *"Go and ask them"* (1 Corinthians 15:3-8).
- **Paul** told a king that these things were "not done in a corner" (Acts 26:26).
- **John** wrote down Jesus' signs *"so that you may believe"* (John 20:31).

Paul even stakes everything on one event: *"If Christ has not been raised, then our
preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain"* (1 Corinthians 15:14). Christianity
doesn't hide from investigation — it invites it.

This matters in conversations. When someone says, *"You just have faith,"* you can
gently reply, *"Actually, I believe because of good reasons. Can I share one?"*

## So Why Doesn't Everyone Believe?

If the evidence is good, why isn't the whole world Christian? Because belief isn't only
an intellectual matter. The Bible gives several reasons:

- **Sin affects our thinking.** Paul says people "suppress the truth" about God (Romans
  1:18). We aren't neutral observers; part of us doesn't *want* God to be real, because
  then He would be in charge.
- **The heart resists.** Jesus said people love darkness rather than light, because
  their deeds are evil (John 3:19-20).
- **Spiritual blindness.** "The natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit
  of God" (1 Corinthians 2:14).

This is why evidence is **important but not enough**. You can walk someone right up to
the door — but someone has to open their eyes to see it.

## The Holy Spirit Does What We Can't

Jesus promised that when the Holy Spirit comes, *"he will convict the world concerning
sin and righteousness and judgment"* (John 16:8). Notice what He convicts people about:
**sin** (we've broken God's Law), **righteousness** (God's perfect standard, seen in
Jesus), and **judgment** (a day of reckoning is coming). That's the heart of the gospel
message. When you share the Law and the cross, you're speaking the very truths the
Spirit uses.

The Spirit usually works **through** means, not around them. When Paul spoke by a river
outside Philippi, *"the Lord opened [Lydia's] heart to pay attention to what was said
by Paul"* (Acts 16:14). Paul spoke. God opened. Both mattered.

The Spirit also works in believers. Romans 8:16 says the Spirit bears witness with our
spirit that we are God's children. Philosopher William Lane Craig has put this helpfully
as the difference between **knowing** and **showing**: a Christian can *know* the faith
is true through the Spirit's inner witness, and can *show* it's true to others by giving
reasons and evidence. We need both.

This takes huge pressure off you. You don't have to be the most brilliant arguer in the
room. You have to be **faithful** — to pray, speak clearly, give reasons, and trust God
with the results.

## Faith, Doubt, and Changing Moods

Here's something that surprises many new Christians: believers have doubts too. A
father who brought his suffering son to Jesus cried out, *"I believe; help my unbelief!"*
(Mark 9:24) — and Jesus healed the boy. Jude tells Christians to "have mercy on those
who doubt" (Jude 22). Doubt is not the opposite of faith; unbelief that refuses to trust
is.

C.S. Lewis made a helpful point about this. Once your reason has weighed the evidence and
accepted that Christianity is true, faith is what keeps you holding on when your
feelings wobble — on a bad day, after a hard conversation, or when life hurts. Your
moods will change. The empty tomb won't.

This is another reason apologetics matters. It isn't only for answering sceptics. It
**steadies believers**. When a doubt hits you at 2 a.m., you can go back to the reasons:
the eyewitnesses, the empty tomb, the changed lives of the apostles, and the Spirit's
witness in your own heart. Faith that knows *why* it trusts is much harder to shake than
faith built on feelings alone.

## One Faith, Several Methods

Christians agree that evidence matters and that the Spirit must work. But they don't all
agree on the best *order* or *starting point* for apologetics. There are four main
approaches — classical, evidential, presuppositional, and cumulative case — and each has
thoughtful defenders. They're laid out fairly in the "Where Christians Differ" section
of this lesson. Read them carefully: you'll probably find that you naturally use pieces
of all four.

Here's a simple way to picture the difference. Imagine a friend doubts that a bridge is
safe. The **classical** approach first shows him the bridge was built by a real engineer,
then shows him this particular bridge. The **evidential** approach walks him straight onto
the bridge and says, "Look — thousands cross it every day." The **presuppositional**
approach points out that he trusted a bridge to get here, so his doubt doesn't fit how
he actually lives. The **cumulative case** approach lays out the engineering, the
inspections, and the traffic history together. Different routes — same bridge, and the
same goal: getting him across to Jesus.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise John 20:31**, and use it whenever someone says faith is "blind."
2. **Write your own one-sentence definition of faith** that a non-Christian friend
   would understand, and practise the plane illustration out loud.
3. **Read 1 Corinthians 15:1-20** and list every piece of evidence Paul mentions.
4. **Before your next spiritual conversation, pray** specifically for the Holy Spirit
   to convict the person of sin, righteousness, and judgment.
5. **Read the "Where Christians Differ" section** and note which approach you lean
   toward — and what you could learn from the others.
