Suppose a friend at church says, *"I'm against abortion personally, but the Bible never
actually mentions it."* He's right that you won't find the word "abortion" in your concordance.
So does the Bible have anything to say?

It has a great deal to say. The Bible doesn't give us a single proof text. It gives us
something stronger: a **consistent picture** of who human beings are, who God is, and how He
relates to people before they are born. When you put the pieces together, the case is clear.

## Built on the Image of God

The foundation is laid on the Bible's first page. Genesis 1:27 says God created mankind **in
His own image**. That's what makes human beings different from animals and plants. We are
valuable not because of our abilities, our usefulness, or our stage of life, but because we
reflect our Creator.

After the flood, God makes the connection explicit. In **Genesis 9:6** He says that whoever
sheds human blood will be held accountable, **because God made man in His own image**. Murder
is not just a crime against a person. It is an attack on the image of God.

Later, God writes it on stone: **"You shall not murder"** (Exodus 20:13). The Hebrew word
refers to the unjust killing of a human being. And throughout Scripture, God especially hates
"hands that shed innocent blood" (Proverbs 6:16-17).

So the key question for Christians is the same as the scientific question from the last
lesson: **Are the unborn human beings?** If they are, they bear God's image, and the command
applies to them.

## God Forms and Knows the Unborn

Now look at how Scripture speaks about life in the womb.

**Psalm 139:13-16.** David praises God: *you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together
in my mother's womb.* He goes on to say God saw his "unformed substance" and wrote his days in
His book before any of them came to be. Notice the pronouns: **me**, **my**. David doesn't say,
"You formed some tissue that later became me." He says God was forming **him**.

**Jeremiah 1:5.** God tells Jeremiah that before He formed him in the womb He **knew** him, and
before he was born He **set him apart**. God is relating to Jeremiah as a person with a calling
before birth.

**Job 31:15.** Job reasons that God made both him and his servant "in the womb" — the same God
fashioned them both, so they share equal dignity. Job uses our shared origin in the womb as a
basis for treating others justly.

> Some object that these passages are poetry. They are! But poetry describes reality. A love
> poem about your wife is still about a real woman. These poems are about real people whom God
> knew and formed before birth.

## The Unborn in the New Testament

**Luke 1:41-44.** When Mary, newly pregnant with Jesus, visits her relative Elizabeth, the
unborn John the Baptist **leaps for joy** in his mother's womb. John, at six months' gestation,
responds to the presence of his Lord.

Luke, a physician, uses a telling word here. He calls unborn John a **brephos** — the Greek
word for "baby." He uses the very same word for the **newborn Jesus** lying in the manger (Luke
2:12, 16) and for the **infants** people brought to Jesus (Luke 18:15). To Luke, a baby is a
baby, inside or outside the womb.

**The incarnation.** Most striking of all, God the Son **Himself** began His human life in the
womb. Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit and grew in Mary's body like every other child.
When Mary arrives, Elizabeth calls her **"the mother of my Lord"** (Luke 1:43) — even though
Jesus is at most a few weeks past conception. God did not skip the embryo stage. He sanctified
every stage of human life by living through it.

## An Honest Look at Exodus 21:22-25

This passage is sometimes raised by people on both sides, so it's worth handling honestly and
briefly. It describes men fighting who accidentally strike a pregnant woman, so that her child
comes out. If there is no further harm, a fine is paid; if there is harm, the penalty is "life
for life, eye for eye."

Christians read it in two main ways:

- **Premature birth.** Many scholars argue the Hebrew describes a **live birth** — the words
  used are those normally used for children being born. On this reading, if mother **or**
  child is harmed, the penalty is the same, which would treat the unborn child as fully equal.
- **Miscarriage.** Others, following some older translations, read it as a miscarriage, with a
  fine for the lost child and "life for life" only if the **mother** dies — suggesting a lesser
  penalty.

Be honest: this passage is debated, and the case for unborn life doesn't rest on it. But notice
that even on the miscarriage reading, it describes an **accidental** injury, not a deliberate
killing, and it still treats the loss as a real wrong requiring a penalty. It gives no support
to intentionally ending an unborn life.

## Putting the Pieces Together

When someone asks where the Bible talks about abortion, you can now show them a clear chain of
reasoning, one link at a time:

1. **Humans bear God's image**, so intentionally taking innocent human life is murder
   (Genesis 1:27; 9:6; Exodus 20:13).
2. **Scripture treats the unborn as human persons** — God forms them, knows them, and calls
   them by the same words used for born children (Psalm 139; Jeremiah 1:5; Luke 1:41-44).
3. **Therefore, intentionally taking unborn life falls under God's command not to murder.**

Notice that this is the same shape as the scientific argument in the previous lesson. Science
tells us the unborn is a living human being. Scripture tells us why every human being matters
— because each one bears the image of God. The two work together.

When you share this, don't fire verses like bullets. Read the passages *with* your friend. Ask,
*"Who is David talking about in Psalm 139?"* or *"What word does Luke use for John here?"* Let
Scripture make the case while you ask the questions.

## The Church Has Always Known

Christians didn't discover this issue recently. In the Roman world, abortion and the exposure
of unwanted newborns were common. The early church was known for refusing both. The **Didache**,
a very early Christian manual, forbids abortion explicitly, and early Christian writers
repeated the same conviction. Christians also rescued abandoned babies and raised them as their
own.

That tells us something important: **being pro-life has always been a gospel issue**. It flows
from believing that every human being is made in God's image — and that God so loved sinners
that He sent His Son to become one of us, beginning in the womb, and to die for us. That same
gospel offers full forgiveness to anyone who has been involved in abortion and turns to Christ.
