Imagine you're holding a photograph of yourself as a newborn. Then one at age five. Then at
fifteen. Then today. Each picture looks different — but it's the same person in every one:
**you**.

Now imagine one more photo: an ultrasound image taken when you were ten weeks old in the
womb. Is that also you?

That question — **what is the unborn?** — sits at the centre of the abortion debate. It is not
the only question, but it is the one that decides all the others. Greg Koukl has made the
point memorably: if the unborn is not a human being, you don't need any reason to justify
abortion — but if the unborn is a human being, no reason is good enough.

## The One Question That Matters Most

Most arguments for abortion are about **difficult circumstances**: "She can't afford a child."
"She's too young." "It's her body." "The baby might be disabled." These are real hardships, and
we should never brush them aside.

But notice something. None of those reasons would justify killing a **two-year-old**. A
mother in poverty cannot end her toddler's life to save money. That tells us every one of these
arguments quietly assumes that the unborn is **not** a human being like the toddler.

So the whole debate comes back to one question: **What is the unborn?**

## What Science Says

You don't need a biology degree to answer this. The basic facts are taught in standard
embryology textbooks. **Embryology** is the science of how living things develop before
birth.

- **Fertilization.** When a sperm and an egg unite, they form a single cell called a
  **zygote**. At that moment a new organism comes into being.
- **Distinct.** The new organism has its own unique genetic code, different from both mother
  and father. Its genetic sex is already set.
- **Living.** It grows, metabolises nutrients, and its cells divide. Dead things don't grow.
- **Human.** It has human DNA and is the offspring of two human parents. Human parents don't
  produce any other kind of being.
- **Whole.** This is the key point. It is not a *part* of another organism, like a skin cell
  or a kidney. It is a complete, **self-directing** organism. Given nutrition and a suitable
  environment, it develops itself from within into the next stages of life.

From there, development is breathtaking. Roughly three weeks after fertilization, the heart
begins to beat. By around eight weeks, all the major body parts and organs are present in basic
form, and the embryo is now called a **fetus** — a Latin word meaning "offspring" or "young
one." Birth, weeks later, changes the baby's location and how she breathes and eats. It does not
change what she is.

> An acorn is an oak tree at an early stage of development. You might not value an acorn as
> much as a majestic oak — but a human embryo isn't valuable because of how she looks. She's
> valuable because of **what she is**: a member of the human family.

It's important to be accurate and not overstate. Science can tell us **what** the unborn is —
a living human organism. It is philosophy and ethics that tell us whether all human beings
deserve protection. But that second step is one almost everyone already accepts: we believe it
is wrong to intentionally kill innocent human beings.

## The SLED Test

So what is the difference between the unborn and you? The philosopher **Stephen Schwarz**
noticed that there are really only four differences, and pro-life apologist **Scott
Klusendorf** popularised them with the memorable acronym **SLED**:

- **S — Size.** The unborn is smaller than you. But a toddler is smaller than a teenager, and
  we don't think the teenager is more valuable. Size doesn't decide worth.
- **L — Level of development.** The embryo is less developed than you. But so is a four-year-old
  compared with an adult. Development continues into your twenties. Being less developed
  doesn't make someone less human.
- **E — Environment.** The unborn is inside the womb; you're outside. But where you are
  doesn't change **who** you are. A short journey down the birth canal doesn't turn a
  non-human into a human.
- **D — Degree of dependency.** The unborn depends on her mother to survive. But newborns
  depend completely on others too, as do people on insulin, dialysis, or ventilators.
  Dependence calls for care, not a death sentence.

Memorise these four letters. In almost every conversation, a person's reason for treating
the unborn differently falls into one of them.

## Trot Out the Toddler

**Greg Koukl** of Stand to Reason teaches a simple way to keep conversations focused. When
someone offers a reason for abortion, ask whether that same reason would justify killing a
toddler. He calls it **"trotting out the toddler."**

- "She's poor." — *Could she end her toddler's life because she's poor?*
- "It's her body." — *Could she end her toddler's life because the child is disrupting her
  freedom?*
- "The child will be unwanted." — *Could we kill toddlers who are unwanted?*

The answer is always no. The point isn't to be clever or dismissive of real hardship. It's to
gently show that the real question isn't "Is life hard?" but "**What is the unborn?**"

## The Argument in Three Lines

Put the science and the SLED test together, and you have a simple argument that Scott
Klusendorf and others have taught for years. It fits on a napkin:

1. **It is wrong to intentionally kill an innocent human being.**
2. **Abortion intentionally kills an innocent human being.**
3. **Therefore, abortion is wrong.**

Almost everyone already accepts the first line — it's the foundation of every law against
murder. The second line is where the science comes in: the unborn is a living, whole human
being, and abortion is designed to end that life. If both lines are true, the conclusion
follows.

This helps you in conversation, because you can ask a friend, *"Which line do you disagree
with?"* That keeps the discussion focused and honest. Most people will challenge the second
line by saying the unborn isn't really human, or isn't a "person" yet. That's exactly where the
SLED test and the toddler question do their work.

It also keeps you humble. You're not asking anyone to trust your feelings. You're asking them
to look at the evidence and follow it where it leads.

## Keep It Simple, Keep It Kind

You don't need to win every argument. Here are some guidelines:

- **Stay on the key question.** Many conversations get lost in side issues. Bring it back:
  "What is the unborn?"
- **Be accurate.** Don't exaggerate facts or use loaded language. The truth is powerful enough.
- **Remember who might be listening.** Many people have been touched by abortion personally.
  Speak as if a post-abortive mother or father is standing right there — because often, they
  are.
- **Point to the Creator.** The wonder of human development points to the God who knits each of us
  together in the womb (Psalm 139:13). The same God who made us offers forgiveness through Jesus to all who repent —
  including those who have taken part in abortion.

   reasons and responding.
3. **Learn three facts** of human development (fertilization, heartbeat at about three weeks,
   all major organs by about eight weeks).
4. **Memorise Proverbs 31:8** and pray it for yourself: that you would speak up for those who
   cannot speak.

   the next lessons.
