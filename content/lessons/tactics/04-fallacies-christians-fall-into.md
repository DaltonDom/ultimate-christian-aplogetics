Every so often, a thrilling story goes viral among Christians: NASA scientists, running
calculations on the positions of the planets, supposedly discovered a "missing day" —
the day the sun stood still for Joshua! Believers repost it by the thousand.

There's just one problem. It isn't true. It's an urban legend that has been circulating
since the 1960s, there's no record of any such discovery, and many Christian ministries
have debunked it themselves.

The same goes for the story that Charles Darwin renounced evolution on his deathbed.
It was popularised by a woman known as Lady Hope, but Darwin's own daughter publicly
denied it, and historians consider it unreliable.

Every time a Christian shares a story like this, a skeptic somewhere notices — and files
it under *"Christians will believe anything."* The last lesson looked at fallacies
skeptics use. This one turns the mirror around.

## Why Our Mistakes Matter

It's tempting to think, *"The other side uses bad arguments, so it's okay if we do
too."* But Christians are held to a higher standard, for three reasons:

- **We serve the God of truth.** The ninth commandment forbids false witness. Twisting an
  opponent's view or exaggerating evidence is a form of it. Job rebuked his friends for
  speaking falsely *on God's behalf* (Job 13:7). God doesn't need our spin.
- **Our credibility carries the gospel.** If someone catches you exaggerating about
  archaeology, why would they trust you about the resurrection?
- **We're commanded to reason fairly.** A "false balance" — a rigged scale — is an
  abomination to the Lord (Proverbs 11:1). That applies to how we weigh arguments.

## Six Traps to Avoid

### 1. Circular reasoning without admitting it

"The Bible is true because the Bible says it's true." To a skeptic who doesn't yet
accept the Bible's authority, this sounds like *"trust me because I say you can trust
me."*

For a believer, trusting God's Word as the highest authority is not foolish — there's
nothing higher to check it against. But in a conversation, **be honest about what
you're doing**. Either say openly that Scripture is your starting point and explain why,
or start with common ground: treat the Gospels as historical documents and look at the
evidence. (Faithful Christians differ on which approach is best — see the note below.)

### 2. Straw-manning evolution and atheism

We hate it when skeptics misrepresent Christianity. We must not do it back.

- **"If we came from monkeys, why are there still monkeys?"** Evolutionary theory says
  humans and today's apes share a common ancestor, not that we descended from modern
  monkeys. Christians hold a range of views on evolution (see the science lesson in the
  apologetics module), but whatever you conclude, describe the theory accurately.
- **"Atheists believe nothing exploded into everything."** Most atheists wouldn't
  recognise that summary. Ask what they actually think.
- **"Atheists can't be moral."** Many atheists are honest, kind, and generous. The
  Christian *moral argument* doesn't say atheists can't behave morally. It asks whether,
  without God, anything can be **really** right or wrong. Those are very different claims.

Test: could the person you're describing read your summary and say, *"Yes, that's what I
believe"*? If not, try again.

### 3. God-of-the-gaps

This is the argument: *"Scientists can't explain X, so God must have done it."* The
danger is that when science does explain X, God seems to shrink.

The Bible presents God as the author of the **whole story**, not just the gaps. Knowing
how a printing press works doesn't mean the book has no author. The strongest
arguments build on what we **do know** — that the universe had a beginning, that its
physical constants are finely balanced for life, that morality feels binding — not merely
on what we don't.

### 4. Overstating the evidence

The evidence for Christianity is strong. You don't need to inflate it.

- Say **"strong evidence"** or **"the best explanation"** rather than "scientifically
  proven."
- Archaeology has **confirmed many details** in the Bible — rulers, places, customs — but
  it doesn't "prove every word."
- **Check before you share.** Deathbed conversions, discovered arks, fake quotes from
  famous scientists — if it sounds too good to be true, look it up first.

### 5. Appeal to emotion

Emotions are God-given, and some truths — like judgment and the cross — *should* move us.
The fallacy is when emotion **replaces** reasons, or pressure **replaces** persuasion:
sad music and guilt to get a quick decision, or "Wouldn't you feel terrible if you're
wrong?" as your only argument. We want people to repent and believe because they see
what is true — conscience agreeing with God's Law — not because we pushed the right
emotional buttons.

### 6. Ad hominem against skeptics

*"He's just angry at God." "Atheists only want to sin." "That guy is an idiot."*

Scripture does say humans suppress the truth (Romans 1:18) — but that describes all of
us before grace, and you can't read a particular person's heart. Even if someone's
motives were mixed, their argument still deserves an answer. Mocking well-known
atheists online may get laughs from Christians, but it teaches unbelievers that we can't
handle their arguments.

> Honesty is like a bank account. Every time you concede a fair point or say "I don't
> know," you make a deposit. Every exaggeration is a withdrawal. You want plenty in the
> account when it's time to talk about the cross.

## When You Catch Yourself

Sooner or later you'll realise, mid-conversation, that you've just done one of these
things. Maybe you repeated a statistic you can't source, or described someone's view
unfairly. What then?

**Say so, straight away.** *"Actually, I'm not sure that's accurate — let me check before
I lean on it."* Or: *"Sorry, that wasn't fair to what you believe. How would you put it?"*

It feels like losing ground, but it almost always gains it. Skeptics often expect
Christians to be defensive and slippery. When you correct yourself without being asked,
you break that stereotype on the spot.

It also helps to prepare ahead of time. Before you use an argument in conversation, ask
yourself three questions:

- **Is it true?** Can I point to a reliable source?
- **Is it fair?** Would the other side recognise their view in how I've described it?
- **Is it proportionate?** Am I claiming more than the evidence actually shows?

If an argument fails any of these, set it aside. You'll have fewer arguments — but the
ones you keep will be ones you can stand behind.

## Honesty Builds Credibility

The most persuasive Christians are often not the ones with the most arguments, but the
ones people trust. You can build that trust with a few simple habits:

- **Say "I don't know"** when you don't — then go and find out.
- **Concede good points.** "That's a fair criticism" is disarming.
- **Admit hard things.** There are difficult passages in the Bible and real failures in
  church history. Acknowledging them shows you aren't hiding anything.
- **Use measured language.** "I think the evidence points to..." is stronger in the long
  run than "It's obvious!"
- **Drop bad arguments quickly.** You'll lose nothing but a weak link.

There's a deeper reason, too. The gospel begins with honesty: admitting that we are
sinners who can't save ourselves. A Christian who can say "I was wrong about that" is
living out the very humility the gospel calls for — and pointing to the One who is always
true.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Audit your arguments.** Write down the three arguments you use most. Is any of them
   a gap, a straw man, or an overstatement? Fix or drop it.
2. **Practise the fairness test.** Summarise an atheist's or evolutionist's view in a way
   they would agree with.
3. **Adopt a "check before you share" rule** for any Christian story or quote online.
4. **Memorise 2 Corinthians 4:2** and let it shape how you argue.
5. **Pray** for the courage to say "I don't know" and "you're right" when it's true.
