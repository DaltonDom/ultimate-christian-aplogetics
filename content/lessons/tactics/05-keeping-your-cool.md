It's 11 p.m. You're scrolling your phone when you see it: someone has replied to your
post about Easter. *"Imagine believing in a zombie carpenter. Grow up."* Three people
have already liked it.

Your pulse quickens. Your thumbs start typing something sharp and clever. It would feel
so good to send it.

Stop. Before you hit send, ask one question: **What do I actually want here — to win,
or to win him?**

## The Goal Is the Person, Not the Win

You can win an argument and lose the person. In fact, it happens all the time. A clever
put-down might get cheers from other Christians, but the person you were talking to walks
away more convinced than ever that believers are arrogant.

Paul gives Timothy a picture of what a servant of the Lord looks like in a heated
conversation (2 Timothy 2:23-26). He should:

- **avoid foolish, quarrelsome arguments** that go nowhere (v. 23),
- be **kind to everyone**, not quarrelsome,
- be **patient** when wronged,
- **correct with gentleness**,
- remember that **God** is the one who grants repentance (v. 25),
- remember that the person opposing him has been **captured** by the devil (v. 26).

That last point changes everything. The angry skeptic isn't your enemy — he's a captive.
You don't shout at a hostage; you try to help him get free.

And here's the freedom in verse 25: **God** gives repentance. You don't have to crush
every objection or produce a conversion. Your job is to be faithful and kind. The
results belong to Him.

## A Soft Answer: Handling Hostility

When someone comes at you with anger, your natural reaction is to match their energy.
Proverbs 15:1 tells us to do the opposite.

- **Lower your voice** (or, online, your tone). Calmness is contagious.
- **Don't take it personally.** Hostility is often aimed at God, the church, or a past
  hurt — you're just the nearest Christian.
- **Look for the story behind the anger.** *"It sounds like you've had a bad experience
  with Christians. Can I ask what happened?"* Many angry skeptics carry real wounds.
- **Apologise for real wrongs.** If Christians have hurt them, say so: *"I'm sorry. That
  wasn't like Jesus."* You're not apologising for truth, only for sin.
- **Stay steady on the truth.** Gentle doesn't mean spineless. You can be soft in tone and
  clear in content.

### Watch your own heart

Keeping your cool isn't only about managing the other person. It's about what's going
on inside you. Often the thing that makes us snap isn't concern for God's honour — it's
wounded pride. We don't like looking foolish.

James says that our anger doesn't produce the righteousness God wants (James 1:20). So
when you feel the heat rising, ask yourself: *"Am I defending Jesus right now, or
defending me?"* A quick, silent prayer — *"Lord, help me love this person"* — can change
the whole temperature of a conversation.

It also helps to remember that you were once on the other side. Before God opened your
eyes, you had your own objections, excuses, and blind spots. Humility makes patience
much easier.

## Mockery and the Two Proverbs

Mockery stings because it isn't really an argument — it's a way of making you look
foolish without engaging what you believe. But remember: Jesus was mocked more than
anyone. Soldiers dressed Him in a robe, spat on Him, and laughed (Matthew 27:27-31). And
when He was reviled, **He did not revile in return** (1 Peter 2:23).

Proverbs 26:4-5 gives two instructions side by side, on purpose:

- **"Answer not a fool according to his folly"** — don't use his methods. Don't trade
  insults or answer sarcasm with sarcasm, or you'll become like him.
- **"Answer a fool according to his folly"** — but don't let a foolish claim stand
  unchallenged as if it were wise. Sometimes a calm reply exposes the problem.

(In Proverbs, a "fool" is someone who scorns wisdom and God — not someone with a low IQ.
And it's a category to discern quietly, never a label to use on someone.)

Wisdom is knowing which verse fits the moment. In practice, that often looks like:

1. **Don't take the bait.** Mockery wants a reaction. Deny it one.
2. **Use a little humour.** Laughing at yourself disarms people.
3. **Ask a sincere question.** *"Seriously though — what do you think happened to
   Jesus?"* Turns a jab into a conversation.

## Rabbit Trails and the Gish Gallop

Some conversations don't get hostile — they just go everywhere. You start on the
resurrection and five minutes later you're on dinosaurs, the Crusades, and whether
aliens exist. That's a **rabbit trail**: a side topic that pulls you away from the main
point. Try: *"Great question — can I park that for a moment and finish this thought? I
promise I'll come back to it."*

A more intense version is the **Gish gallop**: firing off so many objections so quickly
that no one could answer them all. Ironically, the name comes from Duane Gish, a
creationist debater, whose critics accused him of this style. (A good reminder that
Christians can do it too!)

When you face a flood of objections:

- **Don't try to answer everything.** You'll answer nothing well.
- **Name it kindly.** *"You've raised a lot of big questions. Which one matters most to
  you?"*
- **Pick the most important one.** If they won't choose, you choose — ideally the one
  closest to Jesus. If He rose from the dead, many other questions look different.
- **Offer to come back.** *"Those other ones are worth talking about. Let's grab coffee."*

## Online and Trolls

Online conversations are harder: no tone of voice, no facial expressions, and an
audience. A **troll** is someone who posts mainly to provoke a reaction, not to
understand. A few guidelines:

- **Remember the quiet readers.** Many people read without commenting. Your calm, kind
  reply may reach them even if the troll never budges.
- **Never post angry.** If your heart is racing, wait. Pray. Reply later, or not at all.
- **Assume the best** where you can. Blunt text often reads harsher than it was meant.
- **Two or three replies, then stop** — or move to a private message where there's no
  crowd to perform for.
- **Don't feed trolls.** If someone clearly only wants a fight, a short, gracious reply
  (or silence) is better than a long thread.

> "For lack of wood the fire goes out" (Proverbs 26:20). A troll's fire runs on your
> replies. Say what's true, say it kindly, and then stop adding wood.

## When to Walk Away

Jesus said not to throw pearls before pigs (Matthew 7:6). This isn't permission to
write people off, and it's never a label to use on someone. It's a warning: when a
person keeps treating holy things with contempt, pressing on only invites more
trampling. Jesus Himself fell silent before Herod (Luke 23:9).

It may be time to step back when:

- the person only responds with mockery and shows no interest in listening,
- the conversation keeps escalating,
- **you** are losing your temper,
- they have asked you to stop.

When you walk away, do it graciously: *"I've enjoyed this. I don't think we'll settle
it today, but if you ever want to talk more, I'm around."* Leave a door open. Then keep
praying. Walking away from a conversation isn't walking away from a person.

Remember, every one of us was once an enemy of God (Romans 5:10), and He was patient
with us. That patience is exactly what the gospel is about.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise Proverbs 15:1** and pray it before hard conversations.
2. **Prepare one light, humorous reply** to the mockery you hear most often.
3. **Practise the Gish-gallop reply** out loud: "Which one matters most to you?"
4. **Set an online rule**: never post in anger, and stop after two or three replies.
5. **Pray by name** for someone who has mocked your faith — that God would grant them
   repentance leading to a knowledge of the truth.
