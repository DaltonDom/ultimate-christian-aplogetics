Picture two Christians at a barbecue. Their friend Dan, holding a burger, says: *"I
just think religion is a crutch."*

The first Christian launches into a ten-minute speech about the evidence for God. Dan
nods politely, stops listening after ninety seconds, and heads back to the grill.

The second Christian smiles and asks, *"Interesting — what do you mean by a crutch?"*
Dan thinks for a moment. *"I guess... people use it to cope with death."* That answer
opens a door to a real conversation about death, hope, and what Dan himself is
counting on.

Same topic. Same Dan. The difference was a question.

## Jesus, the Master Questioner

If you want to see this skill done perfectly, read the Gospels and watch what Jesus
does with His mouth. He preached, yes — but He also asked question after question.
Depending on how you count, the Gospels record Him asking somewhere around three
hundred of them.

- To His disciples: *"But who do you say that I am?"* (Mark 8:29)
- To a lawyer testing Him: *"What is written in the Law? How do you read it?"* (Luke
  10:26)
- To a rich young man who called Him good: *"Why do you call me good?"* (Mark 10:18)
- To religious leaders trying to trap Him: *"Was the baptism of John from heaven or
  from man?"* (Mark 11:29-30)

Jesus didn't need information — He knew what was in every heart. He asked because
questions make people **think**, **reveal** what they really believe, and let them
**discover** truth rather than simply being told it. If the Son of God used questions
this way, we should too.

## Why Questions Work

A statement often feels like a push. A question feels like an invitation. Here's why
questions are so powerful in real conversations:

- **They lower defensiveness.** "You're wrong" makes people dig in. "What makes you say
  that?" makes them think. Nobody feels attacked by honest curiosity.
- **They gather information.** You can't answer well until you know what the other
  person actually believes. Scripture says answering before you hear is *folly* (Proverbs
  18:13).
- **They keep you in the driver's seat.** In a car, the one holding the steering wheel
  decides where you go. In a conversation, the one asking questions quietly does the
  steering — without being pushy.
- **They take the pressure off.** You don't have to have a brilliant speech ready. You
  only need your next question.
- **They help people see for themselves.** A conclusion someone reaches on their own
  sticks far better than one you hand them.

> Think of a good question like a torch in a dark room. You're not forcing anyone to
> look at anything — you're simply shining light where they can see it.

## The Columbo Tactic

One of the most useful tools for this comes from **Greg Koukl** of Stand to Reason, who
describes it in his book *Tactics*. He calls it the **Columbo tactic**, after a TV
detective from the 1970s. Lieutenant Columbo wore a crumpled raincoat and seemed a bit
absent-minded, but he had a habit of scratching his head and asking "just one more
thing." His harmless-looking questions always ended up exposing the truth.

Koukl's idea, in a nutshell: you can take the initiative in a conversation without
being aggressive, simply by asking carefully chosen questions. Two questions do most of
the work.

### Question 1: "What do you mean by that?"

This question **clarifies**. People often use big, fuzzy words — "religion," "faith,"
"evolution," "truth" — and mean something quite different from what you'd assume.

- "The Bible has been changed." → *"What do you mean by changed? Changed how?"*
- "All religions are the same." → *"In what way are they the same?"*
- "I'm spiritual, not religious." → *"What does being spiritual look like for you?"*

Sometimes this question alone dissolves the objection, because the person realises
they weren't sure what they meant. At the very least, you now know what you're talking
about.

### Question 2: "How did you come to that conclusion?"

This question asks for **reasons**. It politely reminds the other person that a claim
needs support. Many people have never been asked why they believe what they believe.
Their view came from a teacher, a video, or a friend, and they've never examined it.

Soften it for everyday conversation:

- *"What makes you say that?"*
- *"What convinced you of that?"*
- *"Have you always thought that, or did something change your mind?"*

The last one is gold. It often opens a personal story — a painful church experience, a
loss, a hard question nobody answered. Now you're talking to a person, not debating a
position.

### Going further: questions that lead

Once you understand someone's view and reasons, you can use questions to **gently point
out a problem** or **point toward the truth**. "If the universe had a beginning, what do
you think caused it?" or "If there's no God, where do you think right and wrong come
from?" A leading question lets them walk up to the idea themselves rather than being
shoved.

## Asking Well

Questions are powerful, which means they can be used badly. A few guidelines:

- **Be genuinely curious.** If you're asking only to set a trap, people will sense it.
  The Pharisees asked Jesus plenty of questions, but they were fishing for ammunition
  (Matthew 22:15). Ask because you actually want to understand.
- **Listen to the answer.** Don't spend their reply loading your next line. Listening is
  a form of love.
- **Keep it short.** One simple question at a time. Long, stacked questions feel like a
  cross-examination.
- **Watch your tone.** "What do you mean by that?" can sound warm or sarcastic. Smile.
  Relax. Sound like a friend, not a lawyer.
- **Don't hide behind questions.** If someone asks what you believe, tell them plainly.
  Questions aren't a way to dodge; they're a way to understand.

### When they turn the questions on you

Sometimes a skeptic will use the same tactic back: *"What do you mean by God?"* or *"How
did you come to that conclusion?"* Don't panic — that's a good sign. It means you're
having a real two-way conversation.

Answer plainly and briefly. *"By God I mean the Creator of everything, who is perfectly
good and who made us to know Him."* *"I came to that conclusion partly through the
evidence for Jesus' resurrection, and partly because the Bible describes me more
accurately than anything else I've read."* Then hand the conversation back with another
question: *"Does that make sense? What do you think?"*

If you don't know the answer, say so. *"I've never thought about that. Let me look into
it and get back to you"* is honest, and it gives you a natural reason for a second
conversation. Being willing to be questioned is part of what makes your own questions
feel fair rather than sneaky.

## Questions Lead to the Gospel

Questions aren't the destination. Paul asks, *"How are they to hear without someone
preaching?"* (Romans 10:14). At some point you must **tell** — clearly, simply — about
sin, the cross, and the call to repent and believe.

But questions are how you earn the right to be heard, and how you find where to start.
Some of the best questions move a conversation from the surface to the soul:

- *"What do you think happens when we die?"*
- *"If God were real, would you want to know Him?"*
- *"Do you think you're a good person?"* (which leads naturally into the Good Person
  Test, covered in the evangelism module)

Ask well, listen well, and you'll find that people often open the door to the gospel
themselves.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the two Columbo questions** until they come out automatically: "What do
   you mean by that?" and "How did you come to that conclusion?"
2. **Practise in low-stakes conversations.** Next time someone shares an opinion about
   anything — a film, politics, sport — ask a clarifying question before responding.
3. **Read Mark 11:27-33** and notice how Jesus handled a hostile question with a
   question.
4. **Write down three "surface-to-soul" questions** you could use to turn an ordinary
   chat toward spiritual things.
5. **Pray** for a curious, loving heart — the kind that asks because it genuinely cares
   about the person in front of it.
