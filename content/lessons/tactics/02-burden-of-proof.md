A friend at work says, casually and confidently, *"There is no God."* Most Christians
feel an instant jolt of pressure: *"Now I have to prove God exists — right here, next to
the printer."*

But hold on. Who just made a claim?

Your friend did. And that changes the whole shape of the conversation. This lesson gives
you three simple tools for moments like this: **burden of proof**, **defining terms**,
and spotting **self-refuting statements**.

## The Burden of Proof

The **burden of proof** is the responsibility to support a claim with reasons. The rule
is simple:

> **Whoever makes the claim carries the burden.**

Think of a courtroom. The prosecutor who says "this man is guilty" has to bring
evidence. The defendant isn't required to prove his innocence from scratch; it's the
person making the accusation who has to back it up.

It doesn't matter who speaks first, who is louder, or whose view is more popular. If
someone asserts that something is true — or false — they've picked up a burden.

This cuts both ways, and that's important:

- **When Christians make claims**, we carry the burden. "God exists." "Jesus rose from
  the dead." "The Bible is reliable." We should be ready to give reasons (1 Peter 3:15)
  — and we have good ones.
- **When skeptics make claims**, they carry the burden too. "There is no God." "The
  Bible is full of errors." "Science has disproved religion." "Jesus never existed."
  Those aren't neutral statements. They're claims, and it's fair to ask for reasons.

Burden of proof is **not** a trick for Christians to dodge questions. It's a way of
keeping the conversation fair, so one person isn't doing all the heavy lifting while the
other simply throws out bold statements.

### What does it take to meet a burden?

Carrying the burden of proof doesn't mean producing **absolute, mathematical
certainty**. Almost nothing in life works that way. You can't mathematically prove that
your grandmother existed or that Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon, yet you rightly
believe both.

Most of the time, meeting the burden means offering **good reasons** — evidence and
arguments that make a claim **more reasonable than the alternatives**. That's the
standard a jury uses: not "beyond all possible doubt," but "beyond reasonable doubt."

This is freeing. You don't need to answer every question in the universe before you can
say, "I think Christianity is true." You need good reasons, honestly presented. And the
same fair standard applies to the skeptic: if they claim there's no God, they don't need
certainty either, but they do need reasons.

## Handing the Burden Back — Gently

When a skeptic makes a claim, the easiest way to hand the burden back is a question
(remember the Columbo tactic from the last lesson):

- "There's no God." → *"How do you know that?"*
- "The Bible is full of errors." → *"Which one do you have in mind?"*
- "Science has disproved God." → *"Which discovery disproves Him?"*
- "Religion causes all the wars." → *"What makes you think so?"*

Often you'll find the person hasn't thought it through; they've absorbed a slogan. That's
okay. Don't gloat. Your goal is simply to move from slogans to substance.

### A fair distinction: "I don't believe" vs "There is no God"

Many atheists today describe atheism as a **lack of belief** in God rather than a claim
that God doesn't exist. That's a real distinction, and you should respect it. Someone
who says "I'm not convinced" isn't making a claim about the universe; they're describing
where they are.

So ask kindly: *"Do you believe there is no God, or are you just not convinced there is
one?"* If it's the second, great — the conversation becomes, "What would it look like to
look at the evidence together?" If it's the first, they've made a claim, and it's
reasonable to ask how they know.

## Defining Slippery Words

Many conversations go nowhere because two people are using the same word to mean
different things. Before you argue, **define**. Here are five words that cause the most
confusion:

- **Faith.** Many people think faith means "believing something without evidence" or
  even "despite the evidence." That's not the biblical meaning. In Scripture, faith is
  **trust** — relying on God because of who He is and what He's done. The apostles
  pointed to things they had seen and heard, especially the risen Jesus (1 Corinthians
  15:3-8; 1 John 1:1-3).
- **Evidence.** Ask: *"What would count as evidence for you?"* Some people will only
  accept a lab experiment, but most of what we know — history, law, personal
  relationships — rests on testimony, documents, and the best explanation of the facts.
- **Religion.** Some use it to mean "any belief about God"; others mean "rules and
  rituals." Clarify which. Many Christians agree that Christianity isn't about
  performing religious duties to earn God's favour, but about God rescuing us by grace.
- **Tolerance.** The older meaning was **putting up with views you disagree with** while
  treating the people who hold them with respect. The newer meaning is often **treating
  all views as equally valid**. But notice: you can only tolerate something you
  *disagree* with. And the person who says "you're intolerant for saying Jesus is the
  only way" is not tolerating your view either.
- **Science.** **Science** is a wonderful method for studying the natural world by
  observation and testing. **Scientism** is the philosophical belief that science is
  the *only* way to know anything. Christians love science — many of its founders were
  believers. What we question is scientism.

When someone uses one of these words, ask, *"What do you mean by that?"* You may find
you agree more than you thought — or that the disagreement is somewhere else entirely.

## Statements That Refute Themselves

Some claims have a hidden flaw: they **fail their own test**. Philosophers call these
**self-refuting** statements. Greg Koukl nicknames the tactic of spotting them
"Suicide," because the claim kills itself. Here are some you'll hear:

- **"There is no truth."** — *Is that true?*
- **"You can't know anything for certain."** — *Are you certain of that?*
- **"Only science can give us knowledge."** — *Which scientific experiment proved that?*
- **"It's wrong to judge other people."** — *Aren't you judging me for judging?*
- **"You shouldn't force your morality on others."** — *Isn't that a moral rule you're
  asking me to follow?*
- **"All religions are true."** — *Even ones that contradict each other? Islam says Jesus
  wasn't crucified; Christianity says He was.*

Point these out **gently**. The aim isn't to make someone feel stupid; it's to help them
see that they, like you, actually believe some things are really true and some things are
really wrong. That's common ground — and common ground is where good conversations grow.

> A self-refuting statement is like a man sawing off the branch he's sitting on. You
> don't need to push him. Just point, kindly, at the saw.

## Why This Matters for the Gospel

These tools aren't about winning debates. They clear away fog. When someone realises
that "there's no truth" can't be true, they're closer to asking, *"Then what is true?"*
When they see that biblical faith is trust grounded in real events, they're ready to
hear about the most important event of all: Jesus dying for sinners and rising again.

And when someone admits that some things really are wrong, they're one step from
recognising that *they* have done wrong things — which is exactly where the Law and the
gospel meet them.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise the rule**: whoever makes the claim carries the burden. Practise saying,
   "How do you know that?" in a friendly tone.
2. **Learn the five slippery words** — faith, evidence, religion, tolerance, science —
   and write a one-sentence definition of each in your own words.
3. **Collect self-refuting slogans.** Listen for them this week in conversations, songs,
   or social media, and write down the one-question reply.
4. **Check your own claims.** When you say something about God or the Bible, be ready with
   at least one reason. Carry your own burden gladly.
5. **Pray** for humility, so these tools serve people rather than your pride.
