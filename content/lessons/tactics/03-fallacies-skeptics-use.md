Have you ever walked away from a conversation thinking, *"Something was wrong with what
they said, but I couldn't put my finger on it"*?

Often what you sensed was a **fallacy** — a mistake in reasoning that makes an argument
*sound* stronger than it is. You don't need to memorise a textbook of Latin names. A few
common ones come up again and again in conversations about God, and once you learn to
spot them, you'll feel far more relaxed.

This lesson covers eight. (For a fuller list, see the fallacies reference in the app.)

## First, Two Ground Rules

**1. Ask, don't label.** Saying "That's an ad hominem!" can sound smug, and most people
won't know what you mean. Instead, ask a question that helps them see the gap for
themselves. Every fallacy below comes with a gentle question you can use.

**2. A bad argument doesn't mean a false conclusion.** If someone argues badly for
something, their point might still be true — or there might be a better argument
they haven't thought of. Pointing out a fallacy clears the ground; it doesn't win the
day. Stay humble.

**3. Fallacies are about arguments, not people.** Everyone commits them sometimes —
including you (as the next lesson will show). Spotting one doesn't mean the other person
is dishonest or unintelligent. Most fallacies are simply shortcuts our minds take when
we're in a hurry or feeling strongly.

Here is a simple pattern you can use for almost any fallacy:

1. **Restate** what they said, fairly: *"So you're saying..."*
2. **Ask** a question that exposes the gap: *"Does that follow?"*
3. **Return** to the real issue: *"Can we look at the actual evidence?"*

## Eight Fallacies You'll Actually Meet

### 1. Straw man

**What it is:** Attacking a weak, distorted version of someone's view instead of the
real thing — like beating up a scarecrow and claiming you beat the farmer.

**How it sounds:** "Christians believe in a sky daddy who hates fun." "Faith means
believing things you know aren't true."

**Gentle question:** *"That's not quite what I believe. Can I tell you how I'd put it,
and then you can tell me what you think?"*

### 2. Ad hominem (attacking the person)

**What it is:** Dismissing an argument by attacking the person making it, rather than
the argument itself.

**How it sounds:** "You're only saying that because you're a fundamentalist." "Of
course a pastor would say that." "Christians are hypocrites, so why listen?"

**Gentle question:** *"You might be right about me — I'm far from perfect. But even if I
were a total hypocrite, would that make the argument wrong?"* (Christians' failures are
real, and it's worth owning them. But they show we need a Saviour, not that there isn't
one.)

### 3. Genetic fallacy

**What it is:** Judging a belief as true or false based on **where it came from**
rather than the reasons for it.

**How it sounds:** "You're only a Christian because you grew up in the West." "Religion
started as a way for primitive people to explain thunder."

**Gentle question:** *"That might explain how I first heard about it — but does it tell
us whether it's true?"* After all, you probably learned that the earth orbits the sun
from your teachers too. That doesn't make it false.

### 4. Begging the question (circular reasoning)

**What it is:** Assuming the very thing you're trying to prove. (It doesn't mean
"raising a question" — it means *sneaking the conclusion into your starting point*.)

**How it sounds:** "Miracles can't happen, so the resurrection didn't happen." Notice the
conclusion was decided before any evidence was examined.

**Gentle question:** *"How do you know miracles can't happen? Isn't that the very thing
we're trying to find out? Could we look at the evidence for this event on its own
terms?"*

### 5. False dichotomy (either/or)

**What it is:** Presenting only two options when there are more — or when both could be
true.

**How it sounds:** "You either believe in science or in God." "Either God stops all
suffering or He doesn't care."

**Gentle question:** *"Why can't both be true?"* Pioneers of modern science like
Kepler, Boyle, and Faraday saw their work as exploring God's creation. Science tells us
*how* nature works; it doesn't tell us whether nature has a Maker.

### 6. Appeal to popularity (or to the crowd)

**What it is:** Arguing that something is true because many people — or many smart
people — believe it.

**How it sounds:** "Nobody believes that anymore." "Most educated people are atheists."

**Gentle question:** *"Is it true because lots of people believe it — or do they believe
it because it's true? What are the actual reasons?"* (Be fair: listening to experts is
often wise. The fallacy is treating a head count as if it settles the question.)

### 7. Special pleading — and "Who made God?"

**What it is:** Applying a standard to others while exempting yourself, without a good
reason for the exception.

This one needs care, because the famous question **"Who made God?"** is often used to
accuse *Christians* of special pleading: "You say everything needs a cause, but then you
make an exception for God!"

The honest answer is that careful Christian arguments (like William Lane Craig's
**kalam argument**) don't say *everything* needs a cause. They say **everything that
begins to exist** needs a cause. The universe began; God, by definition, never began. So
He isn't an exception invented to escape the rule — He's outside the rule's scope from
the start. Treat the question as a fair one, not a gotcha.

Where skeptics can slip into special pleading is in **double standards**:

- Accepting an eternal, uncaused universe (or multiverse) while insisting an eternal,
  uncaused God is absurd.
- Accepting ancient historians' accounts of Caesar without fuss, but demanding
  impossible standards of the Gospels.

**Gentle question:** *"Are we using the same standard for both? What would it take for
the Gospels to count as good history?"*

### 8. Chronological snobbery

**What it is:** Assuming an idea is wrong simply because it's old, or that people in the
past were gullible because they lived long ago. C.S. Lewis gave it this name, crediting his
friend Owen Barfield with exposing the habit in his own thinking.

**How it sounds:** "People back then believed in miracles because they didn't
understand science."

**Gentle question:** *"Do you think people in the first century knew that dead men
don't rise and virgins don't have babies?"* They certainly did. That's exactly why the
disciples doubted the first reports of the empty tomb (Luke 24:11) and why Joseph
planned to break off his engagement to Mary (Matthew 1:19). Miracles were surprising
*because* people knew how the world normally works.

> Spotting a fallacy is like spotting a crack in a bridge. You don't need to blow up
> the bridge — just point to the crack, and offer a sturdier way across.

## From Fallacies to the Real Issue

Behind many fallacies is a real person with a real reason for pushing back — a bad church
experience, a painful loss, a desire to live life on their own terms. Your job isn't to
collect "wins" but to help remove obstacles so the person can see Jesus clearly.

So after you've gently addressed the faulty reasoning, move the conversation forward:
*"Can I tell you the main reason I believe?"* or *"What do you think happens after we
die?"* The gospel is the destination. Clearing up bad arguments just helps you get there.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Learn the eight names and one-line definitions** using the flashcards. Don't worry
   about Latin — plain English is fine.
2. **Memorise one gentle question for each.** These are what you'll actually use.
3. **Watch a debate or read a comment thread** this week and try to spot three of these
   fallacies. Then check yourself — did the Christian side commit any? (That's next
   lesson.)
4. **Practise answering "Who made God?"** out loud in under thirty seconds.
5. **Pray** that God would make you wise as a serpent and innocent as a dove.
