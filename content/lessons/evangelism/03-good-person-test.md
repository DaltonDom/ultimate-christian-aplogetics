Most people you meet believe they're basically good. And if there is a heaven, they
assume they'll be fine. That belief is often the biggest thing standing between them
and the gospel, because people who think they're healthy don't look for a doctor.

The **Good Person Test** is a simple set of questions that gently shows a person the
truth about their heart, usually in about three to five minutes. It was popularised by
**Ray Comfort and the Living Waters team** (famously in *The Way of the Master* with
Kirk Cameron), but it's really just a way of doing what Jesus did with the rich young
ruler: holding up God's commandments as a mirror (Mark 10:17-22).

This lesson walks you through each step: **what to say, why you say it, and how to keep
it friendly.**

## Why It Works

Two truths make this approach powerful:

- **Everyone has a conscience.** Paul says God's law is written on human hearts and the
  conscience bears witness to it (Romans 2:14-16). *Conscience* literally means "with
  knowledge." Deep down, people know lying and stealing are wrong. You aren't
  introducing a foreign idea; you're waking up something already there.
- **It uses God's standard, not yours.** You're not giving your opinion about them.
  You're asking how they measure up to the Ten Commandments. The authority is God's,
  and the verdict comes from their own mouth.

Because you ask rather than accuse, the person does the thinking. That's why this
approach rarely turns into an argument when it's done kindly.

## Before You Start: Ask Permission

Most of the time you'll arrive at the test from a transition question (see the previous
lesson). Someone says *"I'm a pretty good person"* or *"I'd hope I'd go to heaven."* Then
ask:

> *"Would you mind if I asked you a few questions to see if you'd pass God's standard?
> It only takes a couple of minutes, and I'll answer them too."*

Asking permission turns a lecture into a conversation. It also means that if they say
yes, they've chosen to hear you out.

## The Questions, One at a Time

**Step 1: "Do you think you're a good person?"**
*Why:* This reveals what they're trusting in. Nearly everyone says yes, which is exactly
what Proverbs 20:6 predicts. Don't argue with their answer. Just accept it and test it.

**Step 2: "Have you ever told a lie?"** Then: *"What does that make someone who tells
lies?"*
*Why:* Almost everyone admits to lying, so it's an easy, honest starting point. Asking
*"What does that make you?"* lets them say the word *liar* themselves. If they say *"a
human,"* smile and say, *"True, but what's the word for someone who lies?"*

**Step 3: "Have you ever stolen anything, even something small?"**
*Why:* People often shrug off small thefts: pens, sweets, downloads. Point out gently
that the **value doesn't change what it is**. Taking a pen or a car both make you a
thief; one is just a bigger theft.

**Step 4: "Have you ever used God's name in vain, as a swear word?"**
*Why:* This shows that sin is **personal**, an offence against God Himself. Explain
briefly: *"That's using the name of the One who gave you life as a word to express
disgust. The Bible says God won't hold that person guiltless."* Many people have never
considered this, and it often lands hard.

**Step 5: "Jesus said that whoever looks with lust has committed adultery in their
heart. Have you ever done that?"**
*Why:* This moves the conversation from actions to the **heart**. God doesn't just see
what we do; He sees what we want (Matthew 5:27-28). Use discretion: with a child, or
where the topic would be inappropriate, use hatred instead: *"The Bible says whoever
hates their brother is a murderer at heart"* (1 John 3:15).

## Landing the Plane: Verdict, Destination, Concern

Now draw it together with three questions. Go slowly here. These are the most
important moments.

**Step 6: Summarise, and include yourself.**
*"So, by your own admission, you've lied, stolen, used God's name as a curse word, and
committed adultery in your heart. I've done every one of those too."*
*Why:* Hearing it together helps the weight sink in. Including yourself keeps it humble.

**Step 7: "If God judged you by that standard on Judgment Day, would you be innocent or
guilty?"**
*Why:* Now they pronounce the verdict. Most people honestly say *"guilty."* If they say
*"innocent,"* ask kindly, *"But you just told me you've lied and stolen. Would a good
judge say you're innocent?"*

**Step 8: "Would you go to heaven or hell?"**
*Why:* The destination follows from the verdict. Many will say *"heaven, because God is
good"* or *"I've done more good than bad."* That's normal, and the next lesson is all
about handling those answers.

**Step 9: "Does that concern you?"**
*Why:* This is the hinge of the conversation. Their answer shows whether they're ready
for grace. If they're concerned, they are ready to hear about the cross (lesson 5). If
they're not, gently stay with the reality of Judgment Day a little longer, or leave them
with something to think about.

## When the Answers Surprise You

Not every conversation follows the script, and that's fine. A few common variations:

- **"No, I'm not a good person."** This is rare, and it's a gift. The person already
  knows their need. Ask what makes them say that, listen with compassion, and you may be
  able to move quickly to the cross. Remember: grace to the humble.
- **"I don't believe in God."** You can still use the test. Ask, *"If there is a God and
  He judged you by these standards, would you be innocent or guilty?"* The conscience
  works whether or not someone believes in God. Their sense that lying and stealing are
  wrong is itself a clue that the moral law is real (see the Apologetics lesson on
  morality).
- **"I'm already religious."** Many churchgoers, and people of other faiths, are
  trusting their good deeds or religious practices. The test lovingly shows that we all
  need a Saviour, not a better score. Ask, *"What are you trusting in to be right with
  God?"*
- **Tears or silence.** Don't rush to fill the quiet. The person may be seeing their sin
  clearly for the first time. Gently ask, *"What are you thinking?"*

However it goes, keep the same aim: help them see their need, and then show them the
One who meets it.

## Keeping It Friendly

The test can be done in a cold, mechanical way, and that does harm. It can also be done
with warmth that people remember for years. The difference is almost entirely tone.

- **Smile and keep it light where you can.** It's a serious topic, but you can still be
  pleasant company.
- **Say "we" and "me too."** You're a forgiven sinner, not a prosecutor.
- **Don't rush.** Pause after the big questions. Silence gives the conscience room to
  work.
- **Listen and adapt.** If someone brings up their own guilt, lean into that. If a
  commandment isn't landing, move on. You don't have to use every step.
- **Keep the goal in view.** The Law is the needle; the gospel is the thread. You never
  stop at the Law. You're bringing them to the cross, where their guilt can be
  completely forgiven.

## Putting It Into Practice

1. **Memorise Romans 3:23** and the eight-word summary on the flashcards.
2. **Write the nine steps on a card** and keep it in your wallet or phone until you
   know them by heart.
3. **Practise on a Christian friend** who plays a friendly unbeliever. Then swap.
4. **Take the test yourself, honestly.** Let it remind you how much you've been forgiven,
   and let that gratitude fuel your witness.
5. **Try it for real once this week**, starting with, *"Do you think you're a good
   person?"*
